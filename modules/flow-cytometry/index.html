    How a flow cytometer lines cells up in a stream, measures each one with light in a few microseconds, separates a dozen or forty colors that overlap, sorts the cells it wants into droplets, and counts the rare ones that decide a diagnosis.

    Prologue

    A microscope that never stops

    A microscope shows a few hundred cells in a field of view. A blood sample holds millions of white cells, and the ones that matter, such as a leukemic clone after treatment or a stem cell for a transplant, may be one in ten thousand or rarer. Flow cytometry measures cells in a moving stream instead of on a slide, one after another, at thousands to tens of thousands per second.

    The figure above shows the whole instrument in one picture. A narrow stream of sample is wrapped in a faster stream of saline, the sheath, which squeezes the cells into single file. Each cell crosses a focused laser beam in a few microseconds. Detectors record the light it scatters and the fluorescence of the dyes attached to it, as short pulses. Each pulse becomes a number, each cell becomes a row of numbers, and a sample becomes a table of a million rows, usually viewed as a cloud of dots.

    Every chapter of this volume follows one part of that chain: the fluid that lines the cells up, the light they scatter, the fluorescence that identifies them, the arithmetic that separates overlapping colors, the droplets that sort them, and the statistics that decide whether a rare population is really there.

    Event
    One thing that crossed the laser and triggered the detectors: usually a cell, sometimes debris or two cells together.
    Parameter
    One measured quantity per event, such as forward scatter or the fluorescence in one detector.
    Gate
    A region drawn on a plot to select a subset of events for further analysis.
    Part I

    The instrument

    1953 Chapter 1 · Fluidics

    Lining cells up

    In 1934 Andrew Moldavan described in Science a device to count cells as they passed through a fine capillary on a microscope stage, read by a photoelectric cell. It was never made to work well: cells clogged the capillary, and a tube wide enough not to clog let them pass side by side.

    Two inventions of the late 1940s and early 1950s solved the counting problem in different ways. Wallace Coulter, an engineer in Chicago, noticed that a cell is a poor conductor compared with the salt solution around it. If cells are drawn through a small hole between two electrodes, each one briefly raises the electrical resistance of the hole in proportion to its volume. His patent was granted on 20 October 1953, and the Coulter counter soon replaced counting blood cells by eye. For a particle much smaller than the aperture, the resistance pulse is approximately

    \[ \Delta R \approx \frac{3}{2}\,\frac{\rho\,V}{A^{2}}, \]

    where \(\rho\) is the resistivity of the fluid, \(V\) the particle's volume, and \(A\) the cross-sectional area of the aperture. The pulse measures volume directly, whatever the cell's shape or color.

    The same year, P. J. Crosland-Taylor in London described the trick that every optical flow cytometer still uses. He injected a slow suspension of red cells into the middle of a faster stream flowing through a wider tube. Because the flow was laminar, the two fluids did not mix: the outer stream squeezed the inner one into a thin core, and the cells within it were forced into single file down the center. This is hydrodynamic focusing.

    How thin is the core?

    In laminar flow through a tube of radius \(R\), the speed falls parabolically from the center to the wall, \(u(r) = u_{\max}\,(1 - r^2/R^2)\). The share of the total flow passing within radius \(r\) is \(2x^2 - x^4\), where \(x = r/R\). If the sample supplies a share \(q\) of the total flow, its core radius \(r_c\) satisfies \(2x^2 - x^4 = q\), so

    \[ \frac{r_c}{R} = \sqrt{1 - \sqrt{1 - q}} \approx \sqrt{q/2} \quad \text{for small } q. \]

    The core width grows with the square root of the sample flow. Running a sample faster widens the core, cells wander farther from the center of the laser beam, and identical cells give less identical signals.

    Pulses, and two cells mistaken for one

    A cell crossing the beam produces a pulse whose shape is the beam profile blurred by the cell's own size. Electronics record three numbers from each pulse: its height (peak), its area (integral), and its width. For a single cell, area and height are proportional. Two cells stuck together give twice the area, but if the beam is narrow along the flow, they pass through it one after the other and the height barely rises. Plotting area against height therefore separates single cells from doublets, which matters whenever a doublet would be mistaken for a cell with twice the DNA or with two markers it does not have.

    1965 Chapter 2 · Light scatter

    Size and texture without a stain

    In 1965 Louis Kamentsky, at IBM, and the pathologist Myron Melamed published a "spectrophotometer for ultrarapid cell analysis" in Science. It measured how much ultraviolet light each cell absorbed and how much light it scattered, hundreds of cells per second, to screen cervical smears for cancer. It was the first instrument to measure more than one parameter per cell in flow, and Kamentsky soon added a sorter.

    Light that hits a cell is scattered in all directions. The light scattered at small angles, a few degrees from the beam, is called forward scatter (FSC); its intensity rises with the cell's size, though it also depends on the cell's refractive index and on the wavelength, as Mie's 1908 theory of scattering from spheres describes. Light scattered at about 90 degrees, side scatter (SSC), comes mostly from internal structure: granules, a lobed nucleus, folds in the membrane.

    With just these two unstained measurements, the white cells of lysed blood fall into three clouds: small, smooth lymphocytes; larger monocytes; and granulocytes, whose granules make them scatter strongly to the side. Drawing a gate around a cloud selects that population for every later analysis. Gating, done by hand on two-dimensional plots, became the basic analytic act of flow cytometry.

    1969 Chapter 3 · Fluorescence

    Counting photons from each cell

    Scatter says what a cell looks like; fluorescence says what it is. In 1969 three groups published fluorescence measurements in flow. At Los Alamos, Marvin Van Dilla and colleagues measured DNA stained with a fluorescent dye, using hydrodynamic focusing and an argon-ion laser. In Münster, Wolfgang Dittrich and Wolfgang Göhde built an instrument around a fluorescence microscope, which the company Partec later sold. At Stanford, Leonard Herzenberg's group, with Henry Hulett, William Bonner, and Janet Barrett, sorted living cells according to the fluorescence of antibodies bound to them. Herzenberg called it fluorescence-activated cell sorting, FACS. The field took the name flow cytometry at a conference in Pensacola, Florida, in 1978.

    The photon budget

    A cell carrying a few thousand dye molecules crosses the beam in a few microseconds and emits a few thousand photons toward the collection lens. The detector, usually a photomultiplier tube, converts only a fraction of them into photoelectrons. If a cell carries the equivalent of \(N\) fluorescent molecules (the unit is MESF, molecules of equivalent soluble fluorochrome), the detector produces on average \(QN\) photoelectrons, where \(Q\) is the detection efficiency, plus \(B\) photoelectrons of background from the optics, the sheath, and stray light. Photoelectron counts follow a Poisson distribution, so the variance of the measured signal is

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat N) = \frac{QN + B}{Q^2}, \]

    after the mean background is subtracted. A dim population sits just above unstained cells, and whether the two can be told apart depends on \(Q\) and \(B\) as much as on the dye. Holden Maecker and colleagues at BD defined the stain index in 2004 to compare reagents and instruments:

    \[ \text{SI} = \frac{\text{median}_{\text{pos}} - \text{median}_{\text{neg}}}{2\,\text{SD}_{\text{neg}}}. \]
    1975 Chapter 4 · Many colors

    When dyes spill into each other's detectors

    Monoclonal antibodies, made possible by Köhler and Milstein in 1975, gave flow cytometry an unlimited supply of identical stains, one for each surface protein. Labeling a cell with several antibodies, each carrying a different dye, lets one tube measure several proteins at once. The difficulty is that fluorescent dyes have broad emission spectra. Fluorescein (FITC), which glows green, also puts a substantial share of its light into the orange detector meant for phycoerythrin (PE).

    If \(\mathbf{x}\) holds the true amounts of each dye on a cell and \(\mathbf{y}\) the detector signals, the two are related by a spillover matrix \(S\), measured from single-stained controls:

    \[ \mathbf{y} = S\,\mathbf{x}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = S^{-1}\mathbf{y}. \]

    Early instruments subtracted spillover with analog circuits, one pair of detectors at a time. In 1993 Bruce Bagwell and Earl Adams showed how to compensate any number of parameters by inverting the full matrix in software, which is what every modern instrument does.

    Compensation removes the spillover, not its noise

    Compensation corrects the average, but the photons that FITC sends into the PE detector still carry Poisson noise, and subtraction cannot remove it. After compensation, bright FITC cells have the right PE median and a much wider PE distribution than unstained cells. Mario Roederer described this spillover spreading in 2001, and it explains the funnel shape of compensated plots and the negative values around zero. On a log axis those negative values cannot be drawn, so they pile up on the axis. David Parks, Roederer, and Wayne Moore introduced the logicle display in 2006, linear near zero and logarithmic far from it; the arcsinh scale in these figures behaves the same way.

    Part II

    What it is used for

    1974 Chapter 5 · DNA content

    Reading the cell cycle from a histogram

    A dye that binds DNA in proportion to its amount turns a flow cytometer into a cell-cycle counter. Cells resting or in G1 have two copies of the genome; cells in G2 or mitosis have four; cells in S phase, copying their DNA, fall in between. Göhde's first instrument was built for exactly this measurement, and in 1975 Awtar Krishan published a rapid method using propidium iodide, a dye that is still the standard.

    The histogram is never as sharp as the biology. Staining and measurement blur every peak by a coefficient of variation (CV), so G1 and G2 become Gaussians and the S phase blurs into both. In 1974 Phillip Dean and James Jett modeled the histogram as two Gaussian peaks with the S phase distributed between them, each component broadened by the same CV, and fitted the proportions. Their approach, refined over decades, still underlies cell-cycle software.

    The same measurement detects aneuploid tumors, whose cells carry an abnormal amount of DNA. A tumor's G1 peak sits at a DNA index above 1. Whether a near-diploid tumor can be seen at all depends on the CV.

    1965 Chapter 6 · Sorting

    One droplet per cell

    Measuring a cell is useful; keeping it is more useful. In 1965 Richard Sweet at Stanford published a high-speed chart recorder that drew with a jet of ink broken into droplets, each charged and steered by deflection plates. The same year, Mack Fulwyler at Los Alamos joined a Coulter volume sensor to a droplet jet and separated mouse red cells from human ones by size, the first cell sorter. Herzenberg's group replaced size with fluorescence in 1969, and their 1972 paper describing the FACS led to commercial sorters from Becton Dickinson in the mid-1970s.

    How the stream breaks

    A jet of liquid is unstable. In 1878 Lord Rayleigh showed that surface tension amplifies ripples on a cylinder of fluid, and that ripples with a wavelength of about \(4.51\) times the jet diameter \(d\) grow fastest. A sorter shakes its nozzle with a piezoelectric crystal at that frequency, so the jet breaks into uniform drops at a fixed distance below the nozzle. The jet speed comes from the pressure behind it, by Bernoulli's equation, so

    \[ v \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho}}, \qquad f \approx \frac{v}{4.51\,d}. \]

    When a wanted cell is measured, the instrument waits the exact time it will take to reach the break-off point, charges the stream as that drop forms, and the drop is deflected into a collection tube.

    Purity, yield, and Poisson

    Cells arrive at random. If \(\lambda\) is the mean number of cells per drop, the number in any drop follows a Poisson distribution. To keep the sorted tube pure, the sorter aborts a wanted cell when another cell would fall into the same sort envelope of \(k\) drops, which happens with probability \(1 - e^{-\lambda k}\). Running faster sorts more cells per hour but loses a growing share of the wanted ones.

    1981 Chapter 7 · In the clinic

    Counting what matters

    In 1981 Michael Gottlieb and colleagues in Los Angeles described previously healthy young men with Pneumocystis pneumonia. Using the new monoclonal antibodies against T cell subsets, they found that the men had lost their helper T cells, which carry the protein CD4. The disease was AIDS, and counting CD4 T cells by flow cytometry became the way to stage it. In 1993 the US Centers for Disease Control added a CD4 count below 200 cells per microliter to the definition of AIDS. Counts guided treatment for decades, and the need for cheaper counting in poorer countries drove the design of simple, dedicated cytometers.

    Flow cytometry also became central to diagnosing leukemia and lymphoma. The combination of surface markers on the abnormal cells identifies the lineage and stage at which a cancer arose. For stem cell transplants, the number of CD34-positive cells in a collection decides whether there are enough to graft; a protocol published by D. Robert Sutherland and colleagues for ISHAGE in 1996 standardized the count across laboratories.

    Minimal residual disease

    After treatment, the question becomes whether any cancer cells remain. A residual leukemia or myeloma may be one cell in a hundred thousand or a million. Finding it is a counting problem. If the rare population makes up a fraction \(p\) of events, and \(r\) of its events are counted, Poisson statistics give a coefficient of variation of

    \[ \text{CV} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}, \qquad N_{\text{total}} = \frac{r}{p} = \frac{1}{p\,\text{CV}^2}. \]

    Laboratories typically require a cluster of a few tens of events before calling a population present at all. The EuroFlow consortium's "next-generation flow" method for myeloma, published by Juan Flores-Montero and colleagues in 2017, acquires at least ten million cells per sample to reach those depths, comparable to the sensitivity of DNA sequencing methods.

    2009 Chapter 8 · Beyond a filter per color

    Spectral, mass, and imaging cytometry

    For forty years, adding a color meant adding a detector behind an optical filter chosen for one dye. By the 2000s, instruments with several lasers measured eighteen or more colors this way, at the cost of rising spillover and careful panel design. Three newer technologies changed the limits.

    Spectral cytometry records each cell's light across an array of detectors covering the whole spectrum under every laser, instead of one filtered band per dye. J. Paul Robinson's group at Purdue built early spectral systems in the 2000s; Sony released the first commercial spectral analyzer in 2012, and Cytek's Aurora followed in 2017. Each dye then has a full signature, and the instrument solves for the amount of each dye on each cell by least squares, a step called unmixing. If \(M\) is the matrix whose columns are the signatures, then

    \[ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = (M^{\mathsf T} M)^{-1} M^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbf{y}. \]

    Two dyes can be separated even if their peaks fall in the same detector, as long as their signatures differ somewhere. The cost is noise: with equal noise in every channel, the noise of each unmixed dye grows by \(1/\sqrt{1-c^2}\), where \(c\) is the cosine similarity of the two signatures. In 2020 Lily Park, Joanne Lannigan, and Maria Jaimes at Cytek published OMIP-069, the first 40-color fluorescent panel, run on a spectral instrument.

    Mass cytometry, described by Dmitry Bandura, Scott Tanner, and colleagues in Toronto in 2009, labels antibodies with heavy-metal isotopes instead of dyes and measures each cell by vaporizing it in a plasma and weighing its atoms in a time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Isotopes barely overlap, so forty or more markers can be measured, at the cost of a slower rate and of destroying the cells. Sean Bendall, Garry Nolan, and colleagues used it in 2011 to map human bone-marrow development across dozens of markers at once.

    Imaging flow cytometry, commercialized by Amnis in 2005, captures a small image of every cell in several channels, joining the throughput of flow to some of the information of a microscope.

    Data with forty dimensions cannot be gated by hand on two-dimensional plots. Algorithms now do the first pass: SPADE (2011) builds trees of clusters, viSNE (2013) applies the t-SNE map to cytometry, and FlowSOM (2015) uses self-organizing maps, with analysts checking the results.

    Chapter 9 · Today

    Choosing a method

    The basic instrument has not changed since the 1970s: focus the cells, cross a beam, count photons. What changed is how many quantities can be measured on each cell and how much computing goes into reading them. The main forms of the technology now sit side by side.

    MethodMarkers per cellSpeedCells survive?Main limit
    Conventional fluorescenceTypically up to 10 to 20Tens of thousands per secondYes, and can be sortedSpillover and spreading between filtered detectors
    Spectral fluorescenceAbout 40Tens of thousands per secondYes, and spectral sorters existSimilar signatures, autofluorescence, and unmixing noise
    Mass cytometryAbout 40 to 50Hundreds to about a thousand per secondNo, cells are vaporizedSlow acquisition; no scatter; limited by available metal isotopes
    Imaging flowA handful of channels, plus imagesHundreds to thousands per secondYesData volume and lower speed
    Coulter impedanceVolume onlyThousands per secondYesNo identity beyond size
    The common thread

    Every limit in this volume comes down to counting. Cells arrive at random, so coincidences and sort aborts follow Poisson statistics. Photons arrive at random, so dim signals blur and compensation spreads. Rare cells arrive at random, so finding one in a million needs a hundred million events. Better instruments do not escape these limits; they spend more photons, more drops, and more events against them.

    Reference

    Glossary

    Compensation
    Removal of fluorescence spillover between detectors by inverting the spillover matrix.
    Coefficient of variation (CV)
    Standard deviation divided by the mean; for a peak, a measure of its sharpness.
    Doublet
    Two cells measured as one event, recognized by a large pulse area for its height.
    Forward scatter
    Light scattered at small angles to the beam, rising with cell size.
    Hydrodynamic focusing
    Narrowing of a sample stream inside a faster sheath stream in laminar flow.
    MESF
    Molecules of equivalent soluble fluorochrome: a calibrated unit of fluorescence intensity.
    Minimal residual disease
    Cancer cells remaining after treatment at levels too low to see by conventional methods.
    Side scatter
    Light scattered near 90 degrees, rising with internal complexity such as granules.
    Sort envelope
    The number of drops charged and deflected for one sorted cell.
    Spillover spreading
    Extra spread in a compensated parameter caused by photon noise of light spilled from another dye.
    Stain index
    Separation of a stained population from unstained cells, in units of twice the unstained spread.
    Unmixing
    Least-squares estimation of each dye's amount from a cell's full spectrum.
