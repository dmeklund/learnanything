    Prologue

    The great watershed

    An optimization problem asks for the values of some variables that make an objective as small (or large) as possible while respecting constraints: the cheapest diet that meets nutritional requirements, the portfolio with the least risk for a given return, the model that best fits data. Most such problems are hopeless in general. A function can have countless valleys, and a search that finds the bottom of one learns nothing about the others.

    Convex problems are different. The function curves upward everywhere, like a bowl, and the feasible set has no dents. Then any valley is the only valley, as the paths in the figure above show, and there are efficient algorithms that find its bottom and prove they have. In 1993 the mathematician R. Tyrrell Rockafellar put it this way:

    In fact the great watershed in optimization isn't between linearity and nonlinearity, but convexity and nonconvexity.

    This volume follows the ideas that led there: Lagrange's multipliers for constraints, Cauchy's gradient descent, the linear programming of the 1940s and its duality theory, the conditions for optimality that Karush, Kuhn, and Tucker found, Markowitz's portfolios, the interior-point methods of the 1980s, and the convex penalties that find sparse answers in statistics and signal processing.

    Part I

    Calculus, then linear programs

    1788 Chapter 1 · Constraints

    Where the level set touches the constraint

    Calculus finds unconstrained minima by setting the derivative to zero, an idea Pierre de Fermat used in the 1630s. Constraints complicate it: the best point on a curve is usually not where the function's gradient vanishes. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the answer in his Mécanique analytique of 1788, in the course of treating mechanical systems with constraints.

    To minimize \(f(x)\) subject to \(h(x) = 0\), look for points where you cannot move along the constraint and still go downhill. That happens exactly when the gradient of \(f\) is perpendicular to the constraint, which is to say parallel to the constraint's own gradient:

    \[ \nabla f(x^\star) + \nu\,\nabla h(x^\star) = 0, \qquad h(x^\star) = 0. \]

    The number \(\nu\) is the Lagrange multiplier. Lagrange packaged the conditions as the stationary points of a single function, the Lagrangian \(L(x, \nu) = f(x) + \nu\,h(x)\). The multiplier also has a meaning of its own: it is the rate at which the best achievable value changes when the constraint is loosened. Economists call it a shadow price, the value of one more unit of a scarce resource.

    1847 Chapter 2 · Iteration

    Walking downhill

    Lagrange's conditions are equations, and most equations cannot be solved by hand. In 1847 Augustin-Louis Cauchy, wanting to solve the equations of astronomical orbits, proposed a method that needs only the gradient: from the current point, take a step in the direction of steepest descent, and repeat,

    \[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(x_k). \]

    On a bowl-shaped function this always makes progress, but how fast depends on the bowl's shape. If the function is much steeper in some directions than others, measured by the condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\) (the ratio of the largest to the smallest curvature), the steps zigzag across the narrow valley. With the best fixed step size, the error on a quadratic shrinks by a factor of \(\big((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\big)^2\) per step, so the number of steps needed grows in proportion to \(\kappa\).

    Momentum

    In 1964 Boris Polyak added momentum: each step also continues some of the previous step, \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k) + \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})\), like a heavy ball rolling down the valley, whose inertia damps the zigzag. In 1983 Yurii Nesterov found a variant that evaluates the gradient after the momentum step and proved it optimal: no method using only gradients can do better on the worst smooth convex functions. Both reduce the number of steps from order \(\kappa\) to order \(\sqrt\kappa\). Stochastic versions of these methods train nearly every neural network today.

    1947 Chapter 3 · Linear programming

    Corners of a polygon

    A linear program minimizes or maximizes a linear objective subject to linear inequalities. The Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich formulated such problems for planning plywood production in 1939, but his work was little known outside the USSR. In the United States, George Dantzig was trying to mechanize the planning of Air Force logistics. In 1947 he invented the simplex method to solve them.

    The feasible set of a linear program is a convex polyhedron, and a linear objective always attains its best value at a corner. The simplex method starts at a corner and moves along an edge to a neighboring corner that improves the objective, repeating until no neighbor is better. In 1947 Jack Laderman of the National Bureau of Standards used it on George Stigler's "diet problem," the cheapest combination of 77 foods meeting nine nutritional requirements. Nine clerks with desk calculators spent 120 person-days to find the optimum: $39.69 a year, at 1939 prices.

    Linear programming spread through industry and economics within a decade: refinery blending, airline crew scheduling, shipping. Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans shared the 1975 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their contributions to the theory of the optimal allocation of resources. In 1972 Victor Klee and George Minty constructed problems on which the simplex method visits every one of exponentially many corners, but on practical problems it usually takes a modest number of steps, a gap between theory and practice that took decades to explain.

    The figure uses a two-variable example from Frederick Hillier and Gerald Lieberman's operations research textbook. A glass company decides weekly production of two products (in batches) to maximize profit \(3x + 5y\) (thousands of dollars), given limited hours in three plants: \(x \le 4\), \(2y \le 12\), \(3x + 2y \le 18\).

    1951 Chapter 4 · Optimality conditions

    Why convexity makes local global

    Lagrange handled equality constraints. Inequalities, which say a resource can be used up to some limit, need more care: a constraint matters at the optimum only if it is tight. William Karush worked out the conditions in his 1939 master's thesis at the University of Chicago, which went unpublished; Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker found them independently and published them in 1951. For minimizing \(f(x)\) subject to \(g_i(x) \le 0\), the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions require multipliers \(\lambda_i\) with

    \[ \nabla f(x^\star) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^\star) = 0, \qquad \lambda_i \ge 0, \qquad \lambda_i\, g_i(x^\star) = 0. \]

    The last condition, complementary slackness, says that each multiplier is zero unless its constraint is active. For a general problem these conditions are necessary, under mild assumptions, but not sufficient: they hold at every local minimum, every saddle, and every local maximum of the right kind.

    Convexity

    For convex problems, the KKT conditions are also sufficient. The reason is the defining inequality of a convex function: it lies above all its tangent lines,

    \[ f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(y - x) \quad \text{for all } x, y. \]

    If the gradient is zero at \(x\), then \(f(y) \ge f(x)\) for every \(y\): a point where the slope vanishes is a global minimum. Any local minimum of a convex function is therefore global, and any algorithm that reliably goes downhill will find it. Werner Fenchel's 1951 lecture notes and Rockafellar's Convex Analysis (1970) built this into a complete theory.

    Part II

    Duality, risk, and interior points

    Dual Chapter 5 · Duality

    A lower bound that meets the answer

    On 31 October 1947 Dantzig visited John von Neumann at the Institute for Advanced Study to describe linear programming. Von Neumann, who had just finished Theory of Games and Economic Behavior with Oskar Morgenstern, saw immediately that every linear program has a twin, a dual program, and conjectured that their optimal values are equal, as in his minimax theorem for games. David Gale, Kuhn, and Tucker published the first proof in 1951.

    The idea works for any problem. Move the constraints into the objective with multipliers, and minimize over \(x\) without constraints. The result is the dual function

    \[ g(\lambda) = \min_x \Big[f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i\, g_i(x)\Big], \qquad \lambda \ge 0. \]

    For any feasible \(x\) and any \(\lambda \ge 0\), the penalty terms are non-positive, so \(g(\lambda) \le f(x)\). Every value of the dual function is a lower bound on the optimum \(p^\star\). This is weak duality, and it needs no assumptions. The best bound, \(d^\star = \max_\lambda g(\lambda)\), is found by solving the dual problem, which is always concave even when the original problem is not.

    For convex problems, under a mild condition found by Morton Slater in 1950 (some point strictly satisfies the inequality constraints), the bound is tight: \(d^\star = p^\star\), strong duality. This is what makes convex optimization certifiable. An algorithm that holds a feasible point and a dual value whose gap is below \(10^{-8}\) has proved its answer is within \(10^{-8}\) of optimal. For nonconvex problems a gap usually remains.

    1952 Chapter 6 · Quadratic programming

    Risk as a quadratic

    In 1952 Harry Markowitz, a graduate student at the University of Chicago, published "Portfolio Selection." Investors, he argued, care about two things: expected return and risk, measured by the variance of return. A portfolio with weights \(w\) on assets with expected returns \(\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) has expected return \(\mu^{\mathsf T}w\) and variance \(w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w\), a convex quadratic. Diversification works because assets that do not move together have small covariances, so a mix can have less variance than any of its parts.

    The best portfolios for every attitude toward risk solve

    \[ \max_w\ \mu^{\mathsf T}w - \frac{\gamma}{2}\,w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w \quad \text{subject to}\quad \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}w = 1,\ \ w \ge 0, \]

    where \(\gamma\) measures risk aversion. As \(\gamma\) varies, the solutions trace the efficient frontier: no portfolio off it has both higher return and lower risk. Without the constraint \(w \ge 0\), which forbids borrowing assets to sell them short, the problem has a closed-form solution; with it, the problem is a quadratic program, convex but requiring an algorithm. Markowitz developed one, the critical line method, in 1956. He shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. Mean-variance optimization is a building block of investment practice, though estimation error in \(\mu\) makes its raw answers notoriously unstable.

    1984 Chapter 7 · Interior-point methods

    Through the middle instead of around the edge

    The Klee–Minty examples left open whether linear programs could be solved in time polynomial in their size. In 1979 Leonid Khachiyan showed they could, with the ellipsoid method; the news reached the front page of The New York Times, but the method was too slow to use. In 1984 Narendra Karmarkar of AT&T Bell Laboratories announced a polynomial-time method that was also fast in practice, and it too made the front page. His method moved through the interior of the feasible region instead of along its edges.

    The modern form uses a logarithmic barrier. Replace the constraints \(a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i\) by a penalty that grows without bound at the boundary, and weight the objective by a parameter \(t\):

    \[ \min_x\ \ t\, c^{\mathsf T}x - \sum_{i=1}^m \log\big(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x\big). \]

    For each \(t\) the minimizer \(x^\star(t)\) lies strictly inside, and it can be found quickly with Newton's method. As \(t\) grows, \(x^\star(t)\) traces the central path toward the optimal corner. Duality supplies the stopping rule: the point on the central path is within \(m/t\) of optimal, where \(m\) is the number of constraints. Increase \(t\) by a constant factor, take a few Newton steps from the previous point, and repeat; the number of steps grows only slowly with problem size.

    In 1994 Yurii Nesterov and Arkadi Nemirovski showed that the same approach works for any convex problem with a suitable "self-concordant" barrier, including second-order cone and semidefinite programs. Interior-point methods now solve problems with millions of variables and sit inside most commercial solvers alongside the simplex method.

    Part III

    Sparsity and modern practice

    1996 Chapter 8 · Sparsity

    Corners that give exact zeros

    Fitting a model with many possible inputs raises a question that convexity answers elegantly: which inputs matter? Choosing the best subset of \(k\) out of \(p\) variables is a combinatorial problem. In 1996 Robert Tibshirani proposed the lasso, which instead penalizes the sum of absolute values of the coefficients,

    \[ \min_\beta\ \frac{1}{2n}\,\|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda\,\|\beta\|_1, \qquad \|\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|. \]

    The problem is convex, and its solutions have many coefficients exactly equal to zero. The reason is geometric. The equivalent constrained problem asks for the point of a diamond-shaped region \(\|\beta\|_1 \le \tau\) where the loss is smallest, and an expanding elliptical level set of the loss usually first touches the diamond at a corner, where some coordinates are zero. A round constraint \(\|\beta\|_2 \le \tau\) (ridge regression, Hoerl and Kennard, 1970) has no corners, so it shrinks coefficients without zeroing them.

    The same idea appeared in signal processing. Scott Chen, David Donoho, and Michael Saunders's basis pursuit (1998) found sparse representations of signals by \(\ell_1\) minimization, and in 2006 Emmanuel Candès, Justin Romberg, and Terence Tao, and independently Donoho, proved that sparse signals can be recovered exactly from far fewer measurements than classical sampling theory requires. Compressed sensing now shortens MRI scans: modern scanners undersample k-space and reconstruct by convex optimization.

    The regularization path

    Varying \(\lambda\) from large to small traces the lasso path. At large \(\lambda\) every coefficient is zero; as \(\lambda\) decreases, variables enter one at a time. Bradley Efron, Trevor Hastie, Iain Johnstone, and Tibshirani showed in 2004 that the path is piecewise linear and can be computed with least-angle regression; in 2007 Jerome Friedman, Hastie, and Tibshirani showed that simple coordinate descent, which cycles through the coefficients and soft-thresholds each one, is fast enough for problems with hundreds of thousands of variables.

    Chapter 9 · Today

    Write the model, let the solver work

    Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe's textbook Convex Optimization (2004) argued that recognizing a problem as convex is the hard part, and solving it is technology. Modeling languages such as CVX, CVXPY, and JuMP let users write a problem in mathematical notation; a set of composition rules called disciplined convex programming verifies convexity and converts the problem into a standard form for an interior-point or first-order solver.

    • Semidefinite programming. Optimizing over positive semidefinite matrices generalizes linear programming to matrix inequalities. In 1995 Michel Goemans and David Williamson used it to approximate the hard max-cut problem in graphs to within 87.8% of optimal, a landmark in approximation algorithms.
    • Control. Model predictive control solves a convex problem every few milliseconds to choose the next actions of a vehicle or chemical plant. SpaceX's landing rockets compute their descent trajectories with convex optimization on board, building on work by Lars Blackmore and Behçet Açıkmeşe.
    • Distributed and first-order methods. For problems too large for Newton steps, methods such as the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), popularized by Boyd and colleagues in 2011, split a problem into pieces solved in parallel.
    • Nonconvex problems. Training neural networks is not convex, yet stochastic gradient methods from this volume find good solutions anyway. Convex relaxations, in which a hard problem is replaced by a convex one whose solution bounds or approximates it, remain the main tool for proving how good a solution is.

    The common thread

    Every method here exploits the same two facts. A convex function's tangent lies below it, so local information tells you about the whole problem; and every convex problem has a dual whose value bounds the answer from the other side. The first lets an algorithm move confidently downhill. The second lets it prove when it has arrived.

    Glossary

    Barrier function
    A penalty that grows without bound as a point approaches the boundary of the feasible set.
    Central path
    The curve of barrier-problem minimizers x*(t), which leads to the optimum as t increases.
    Complementary slackness
    The KKT condition that each multiplier is zero unless its constraint is active.
    Condition number
    The ratio of a function's largest to smallest curvature; it governs how fast gradient methods converge.
    Dual function
    The minimum of the Lagrangian over the primal variables, as a function of the multipliers; always a lower bound on the optimum.
    Duality gap
    The difference between the primal optimum and the best dual bound.
    Efficient frontier
    The set of portfolios with the highest return for each level of risk.
    KKT conditions
    The first-order optimality conditions for problems with inequality constraints.
    Lagrange multiplier
    A weight on a constraint at the optimum; the sensitivity of the optimal value to that constraint.
    Lasso
    Least squares with an ℓ₁ penalty, which sets many coefficients exactly to zero.
    Linear program
    Optimization of a linear objective subject to linear constraints.
    Simplex method
    An algorithm for linear programs that moves between adjacent vertices of the feasible polyhedron.
    Strong duality
    Equality of the primal and dual optimal values, which holds for convex problems under Slater's condition.
