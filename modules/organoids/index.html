    Budding

    How a single stem cell in a drop of gel grows into a small gut with crypts and villi, how pluripotent cells fold into optic cups and brain tissue, and how these organoids now stand in for patients when doctors choose a drug.

    Prologue

    A small organ in a drop of gel

    Put a single stem cell from the lining of a mouse intestine into a drop of gel, give it three signaling proteins, and in about two weeks it becomes a hollow ball of a few thousand cells with buds growing out of it. Each bud is a crypt, the pocket where the gut's stem cells live, and the hollow center is a gut lumen into which the cells shed their dead. This structure is an organoid.

    The figure above follows that growth over fourteen days. Nobody arranges the cells. They divide, stick to one another, sense the signals around them, and fold into a shape that resembles the organ they came from. Madeline Lancaster and Jürgen Knoblich proposed a definition in 2014 that most of the field uses: an organoid is a collection of organ-specific cell types that develops from stem cells or organ progenitors and organizes itself, through cell sorting and spatially restricted lineage commitment, in a manner similar to the organ in the body.

    Organoids come from two kinds of starting cells. Adult stem cells, taken from a biopsy of gut, liver, lung or tumor, give organoids of that one tissue, made of epithelium only. Pluripotent stem cells, either embryonic or reprogrammed from skin or blood, can be led through the steps of embryonic development to make brain, retina, kidney or gut tissue, often with several cell layers. This volume follows both routes, starting with the physics of cells that sort themselves and ending with organoids used to choose treatments for patients.

    Organoid
    A three-dimensional structure grown from stem cells that contains several of an organ's cell types, arranged in part as they are in the organ.
    Spheroid
    A ball of aggregated cells, often from a cancer cell line, without the self-organized structure of an organoid.
    Niche
    The local environment that keeps a stem cell a stem cell: neighboring cells, matrix, and secreted signals.
    Dome
    The drop of basement-membrane gel, set on the bottom of a culture well, in which most organoids grow.
    Part I

    Self-organization

    1907 Chapter 1 · Cell sorting

    Cells that know where to go

    In 1907 Henry Van Peters Wilson, a zoologist at the University of North Carolina, pressed living sponges through fine silk cloth. The sponge fell apart into single cells and small clumps. Within days the cells crawled together, formed aggregates, and the aggregates rebuilt small functioning sponges.

    Later embryologists repeated the experiment with vertebrate cells. In the 1950s Aron Moscona dissociated chick embryo organs with trypsin and found that mixed cells from two organs reaggregated and then separated by type. In 1955 Philip Townes and Johannes Holtfreter mixed cells from the three germ layers of amphibian embryos. The cells first formed a random clump, then migrated until each layer was in its proper place: ectoderm outside, mesoderm in the middle, endoderm inside, as in the embryo.

    Malcolm Steinberg proposed an explanation in 1963, the differential adhesion hypothesis. Cells that stick to one another with different strengths behave like immiscible liquids such as oil and water. The aggregate moves toward the arrangement with the lowest total interfacial energy, so the most cohesive cells end up inside and the least cohesive cells cover them. Steinberg later showed that aggregates of embryonic tissues have measurable surface tensions and that the tissue with the higher tension is always the one enveloped.

    A model of sorting

    In 1992 François Graner and James Glazier turned the hypothesis into a simulation, now called the cellular Potts model. Space is a grid of pixels. Each cell owns a patch of pixels, and the energy of a configuration is the sum of contact energies along every boundary between different cells plus a penalty for cells that drift away from their preferred area:

    \[ H = \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} J\big(\tau(\sigma_i), \tau(\sigma_j)\big)\,\big(1 - \delta_{\sigma_i \sigma_j}\big) + \lambda \sum_{\text{cells } \sigma} \big(a_\sigma - A\big)^2 . \]

    Here \(\sigma_i\) is the cell that owns pixel \(i\), \(\tau\) is its type, \(J\) is the energy per unit of boundary between two types (low means sticky), and \(a_\sigma\) is the cell's current area. At each step, a random pixel tries to copy the owner of a random neighbor. The copy is accepted if it lowers the energy, and with probability \(e^{-\Delta H / T}\) if it raises it, where \(T\) stands for the random motility of the cells. Steinberg's rule falls out of the energies. Dark cells sort to the inside when \(J_{dd} \lt J_{dl} \lt J_{ll}\), and the two types mix when the heterotypic boundary is cheaper than the average of the two homotypic ones, \(J_{dl} \lt (J_{dd} + J_{ll})/2\).

    Differential adhesion is not the whole story. Cortical tension inside each cell, set by actin and myosin, matters at least as much as adhesion molecules, and later work recast Steinberg's surface tension as a balance between the two. But the lesson that carries into organoid culture is the one the model shows: a mix of the right cell types, given time and freedom to move, finds its own arrangement.

    1971 Chapter 2 · Three dimensions

    Spheroids, gels, and the problem of breathing

    Most cell biology of the twentieth century was done on flat plastic. Cells spread out on it in a single layer, lose their shape and polarity, and stop doing much of what they do in the body. Three developments in the 1970s and 1980s pushed culture back into three dimensions.

    In 1971 Robert Sutherland, J. A. McCredie and W. R. Inch grew cancer cells as free-floating balls, multicellular spheroids, as a model of small tumor nodules. Spheroids grew to a few hundred micrometers across and then developed a dead center surrounded by a shell of living cells, just as poorly vascularized tumors do. The cause was supply by diffusion alone.

    In 1975 James Rheinwald and Howard Green found that human skin keratinocytes, grown on a layer of irradiated mouse fibroblasts, formed stratified sheets resembling epidermis. The method was soon used to grow skin grafts for burn patients. It showed that adult human stem cells could be expanded in culture for long periods if given the right neighbors.

    The third development was a gel. A mouse tumor, the Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm sarcoma, secretes large amounts of basement membrane, the thin sheet of matrix on which every epithelium sits. In the early 1980s Hynda Kleinman and colleagues at the US National Institutes of Health extracted it as a liquid that sets into a gel at body temperature, later sold as Matrigel. It is mostly laminin and type IV collagen. Mina Bissell's laboratory at Berkeley showed in 1989 that mouse mammary cells embedded in it formed hollow spheres that secreted milk proteins into their centers, which they did not do on plastic. Matrix, Bissell argued, is a signal in its own right.

    How large can a ball of cells grow?

    A spheroid or organoid with no blood vessels receives oxygen only by diffusion from the medium. If cells consume oxygen at a constant rate \(Q\) per unit volume, the steady concentration \(C(r)\) in a sphere of radius \(R\) obeys

    \[ D\,\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^{2}\frac{dC}{dr}\right) = Q, \qquad C(R) = C_0, \]

    whose solution is a parabola, \(C(r) = C_0 - \frac{Q}{6D}(R^2 - r^2)\). The center runs out of oxygen when the radius exceeds

    \[ R^{*} = \sqrt{\frac{6\,D\,C_0}{Q}} . \]

    Beyond that radius an anoxic core forms whose edge \(r_0\) satisfies \(C_0 = \frac{Q}{6D}\big(R^2 - 3r_0^2 + 2r_0^3/R\big)\), and cells are already short of oxygen wherever the concentration falls below a hypoxic threshold. The radius grows only as the square root of the oxygen supply, so doubling the oxygen in the incubator buys about 40% more radius.

    The same years brought the cells that would later be grown in these gels. Martin Evans and Matthew Kaufman, and separately Gail Martin, isolated mouse embryonic stem cells in 1981. James Thomson derived human embryonic stem cells in 1998. In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka reprogrammed mouse fibroblasts into pluripotent cells with four transcription factors, and in 2007 they did the same with human cells. Any person's skin or blood could now be turned into cells able to make any tissue.

    Part II

    Two routes to an organoid

    2009 Chapter 3 · Adult stem cells

    A gut from one cell

    The lining of the small intestine is replaced every four to five days. Finger-like villi project into the gut, and between them, pockets called crypts hold the stem cells that resupply them. In 2007 Nick Barker, Hans Clevers and colleagues at the Hubrecht Institute in Utrecht showed that a gene called Lgr5 marks those stem cells: small, slender cells wedged between Paneth cells at the bottom of each crypt. When they labeled Lgr5 cells genetically, the label spread over months to every cell type of the epithelium.

    In 2009 Toshiro Sato, a postdoctoral fellow in Clevers's laboratory, sorted single Lgr5 cells into Matrigel with three proteins: epidermal growth factor (EGF), which drives proliferation; R-spondin 1, which amplifies the Wnt signal that keeps stem cells as stem cells; and Noggin, which blocks the BMP signal that pushes cells toward differentiation. The single cells grew into organoids with crypt-like buds and villus-like domains, containing every cell type of the intestinal epithelium, without any of the connective tissue thought to form the niche. The organoids could be split and passaged for more than a year and remained genetically stable.

    Two years later the same group found that Paneth cells, which sit beside the stem cells, supply them with Wnt3 and EGF. The organoid carries its own niche in its buds. Human intestinal organoids needed a few more factors, including Wnt3a and inhibitors of the TGF-β and p38 pathways, and were reported in 2011. Within a few years the recipe had been adapted to stomach, colon, liver, pancreas, prostate, lung and breast, and to the tumors of each.

    Neutral competition

    Each crypt base holds roughly fourteen Lgr5 cells, and each divides about once a day. Yet a crypt does not grow. In 2010 Hugo Snippert and colleagues labeled single stem cells in four random colors with a reporter called Confetti and watched what happened to the colors over months. Crypts that started with several colors gradually lost all but one. They concluded that stem cells mostly divide symmetrically, that a daughter is pushed out of the niche at random, and that clones win or lose by chance, a process called neutral drift. Carla Lopez-Garcia, Douglas Winton and colleagues reached the same conclusion the same year by a different route. Arranged around the crypt base, the stem cells behave like a ring in which a dividing cell replaces one of its neighbors.

    How many cells are in that ring is disputed. Counting Lgr5 cells gives about fourteen, but fitting the drift model to clone sizes gives fewer: Kozar, Winton and colleagues estimated in 2013 that only five to seven of them function as stem cells at any time, with the rest able to take over if one is lost.

    Neutral drift has a consequence for cancer. A mutation that arises in one stem cell usually disappears by chance. A mutation that makes a cell slightly more likely to replace its neighbors, such as loss of the APC gene, gives its clone a head start in this lottery, and once a crypt is taken over the mutation is fixed in it. Organoid culture made it possible to watch these competitions directly, in a dish.

    2008 Chapter 4 · Pluripotent stem cells

    Eyes, brains and kidneys from scratch

    At the RIKEN Center for Developmental Biology in Kobe, Yoshiki Sasai's group grew mouse and human embryonic stem cells as floating aggregates in serum-free medium. In 2008 Mototsugu Eiraku and colleagues in the group reported that these aggregates formed layered cortical tissue on their own. In 2011 the group showed something more striking. Aggregates of mouse stem cells budded out vesicles that folded inward into two-walled optic cups, with neural retina on the inside and pigment epithelium outside, the same shape the eye takes in the embryo. Human optic cups followed in 2012.

    Other organs followed quickly. In 2011 Jason Spence, James Wells and colleagues in Cincinnati led human pluripotent cells through endoderm and hindgut stages with activin A, FGF4 and Wnt3a. Small spheres budded off the culture and, embedded in Matrigel with the Sato factors, grew into intestinal organoids with both epithelium and surrounding mesenchyme. In 2013 Madeline Lancaster and Jürgen Knoblich in Vienna grew cerebral organoids up to about 4 mm across in spinning bioreactors, with distinct regions resembling forebrain, retina and choroid plexus. The same year Takanori Takebe and colleagues in Yokohama mixed hepatic cells with endothelial and mesenchymal cells and grew liver buds that connected to the host's blood vessels after transplantation into mice. Kidney organoids with nephrons, collecting ducts and blood vessel cells came from Minoru Takasato and Melissa Little in 2015.

    Recapitulating the embryo

    All of these protocols work by giving cells the signals they would meet in the embryo, in the same order. In the embryo, much of that order comes from morphogens, signaling molecules that spread from a source and are degraded as they go. Lewis Wolpert framed the idea in 1969 as the French flag problem: how can a line of cells form three stripes in fixed proportions? A morphogen made at one end, diffusing with coefficient \(D\) and degraded at rate \(k\), settles into an exponential gradient,

    \[ c(x) = c_0\, e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{D/k}, \]

    and cells read their position by comparing the local concentration with thresholds. A boundary forms where \(c(x) = \theta\), at \(x_\theta = \lambda \ln(c_0/\theta)\). If the source strength \(c_0\) varies from one embryo or organoid to the next by a fraction \(\delta\), the boundary moves by about \(\lambda\,\delta\), whatever the threshold. A short gradient is precise; a long one tolerates noise poorly. This is one reason organoids, which lack the embryo's precisely placed sources, vary so much in their regional makeup.

    Organoids reproduce some of the embryo's patterning on their own, through local sources that appear by chance. In 2014 Susanne van den Brink, Alfonso Martinez Arias and colleagues found that small aggregates of mouse stem cells given one pulse of a Wnt activator broke symmetry and elongated along an axis; these gastruloids later formed segments resembling somites. Engineers now place morphogen sources deliberately, with beads, microfluidic channels or light-controlled genes, to impose the gradients that free-floating organoids lack.

    Part III

    Organoids as patients

    2013 Chapter 5 · Cystic fibrosis

    A swelling test

    Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in CFTR, a chloride channel in the surface of epithelial cells. Without chloride flow, water does not follow, and mucus in the lungs, pancreas and gut becomes thick and sticky. More than two thousand CFTR variants are known. Drugs called CFTR modulators help some of them: potentiators such as ivacaftor hold a channel open longer, and correctors help a misfolded channel reach the cell surface. For a patient with a rare variant, there is often no clinical trial to say whether a modulator will work.

    In 2013 Johanna Dekkers, Jeffrey Beekman, Hans Clevers and colleagues in Utrecht grew organoids from small rectal biopsies of people with cystic fibrosis. Healthy intestinal organoids form a closed sphere with the gut surface facing inward. When the researchers added forskolin, which raises cAMP and opens CFTR, healthy organoids pumped chloride and water into their lumens and swelled visibly within minutes. Organoids from patients barely swelled. Adding a modulator restored swelling in proportion to how well it rescued the channel.

    Swelling is measured as the increase in organoid cross-sectional area over an hour, summarized as the area under the curve. A simple model captures it. Fluid enters at a rate proportional to the epithelial surface area \(S\) and to the CFTR activity \(f\) (as a fraction of normal), so the volume grows as \(dV/dt = k f S\). For a sphere, \(S \propto V^{2/3}\), and the radius therefore grows linearly in time, \(dR/dt = k f\), until the wall is stretched to its limit.

    In 2016 the Utrecht group reported that swelling of a patient's organoids under a modulator correlated with that patient's improvement in lung function and sweat chloride. In 2019 Gitte Berkers and colleagues measured organoids from 97 people with cystic fibrosis and used the test to select patients with rare variants who then responded to ivacaftor in the clinic. Dutch health insurers funded a trial to judge whether the test could decide which patients receive modulators, a rare case of an organoid assay entering reimbursement decisions.

    2015 Chapter 6 · Cancer

    Tumors in a dish

    Cancer cell lines, grown on plastic for decades, have drifted far from the tumors they came from. In 2015 Marc van de Wetering and colleagues at the Hubrecht Institute grew organoids from the colorectal tumors of 20 patients alongside organoids from each patient's normal colon. The tumor organoids kept the mutations of the original tumors, and they could be screened against panels of drugs, linking each tumor's mutations to the drugs it resisted. This was the first living biobank; similar ones followed for pancreas, breast, liver, bladder and other cancers.

    Organoids also let researchers build a tumor step by step. The same year, Jarno Drost and colleagues in Utrecht, and Mami Matano, Toshiro Sato and colleagues in Tokyo, used CRISPR to mutate the four genes most often hit in colorectal cancer, APC, KRAS, TP53 and SMAD4, in normal human colon organoids. Each mutation removes the need for one factor in the medium. APC loss switches on the Wnt pathway inside the cell, so the organoid no longer needs Wnt or R-spondin. Activating KRAS makes EGF unnecessary. Losing SMAD4 blinds the cell to BMP, so Noggin is no longer needed. Losing TP53 lets cells survive nutlin-3, a drug that activates p53. Withdrawing a factor therefore selects for the cells carrying the matching mutation. Quadruple mutants grew without any niche factor and formed invasive tumors when transplanted into mice.

    Predicting a patient's response

    The clinical question is whether a patient's tumor organoids respond to drugs the way the patient does. In 2018 Georgios Vlachogiannis, Nicola Valeri and colleagues at the Institute of Cancer Research in London grew organoids from 110 biopsies of 71 patients with metastatic gastrointestinal cancers. Where they could compare the organoid's drug response with the patient's, the organoid test had a positive predictive value of 88% and a negative predictive value of 100%. When the organoid resisted a drug, the patient's tumor did too.

    Those two numbers depend on more than the test. The positive predictive value is the share of patients called sensitive who really respond,

    \[ \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens}\cdot p}{\text{sens}\cdot p + (1-\text{spec})(1-p)}, \qquad \text{NPV} = \frac{\text{spec}\,(1-p)}{\text{spec}\,(1-p) + (1-\text{sens})\,p}, \]

    where \(p\) is the share of patients who respond. A drug that few patients respond to gives a high NPV almost automatically, and the cohorts of early organoid studies were small.

    Organoids take weeks to establish, and not every biopsy grows. Success rates differ by tumor type, from above 70% for colorectal cancer to much lower for others, and normal cells in the biopsy can outgrow the tumor cells. Several prospective trials now test whether choosing treatment by organoid response improves outcomes; the early evidence is mixed, and it remains an open question.

    2016 Chapter 7 · Disease models

    A brain in a dish, and its limits

    Lancaster and Knoblich's first cerebral organoids already modeled a disease. They grew organoids from the cells of a patient with microcephaly, a severely small brain, caused by a mutation in CDK5RAP2. The organoids were smaller, because their progenitor cells stopped dividing and turned into neurons too early. Mice with the same mutation have nearly normal brains; the human outer layers of progenitors that the mutation depletes barely exist in mice.

    In early 2016, during the Zika epidemic in Brazil, several groups infected brain organoids with the virus. Xuyu Qian, Hongjun Song, Guo-li Ming and colleagues at Johns Hopkins, and Patricia Garcez, Stevens Rehen and colleagues in Rio de Janeiro, found that Zika preferentially infected and killed neural progenitor cells and shrank the organoids, connecting the virus to the epidemic of microcephaly within months. In 2020 Mart Lamers and colleagues showed that SARS-CoV-2 infects human intestinal organoids, explaining the gut symptoms of COVID-19. Organoids from human cells answered questions that animals with different receptors could not.

    Variation

    Organoids also vary, from one organoid to the next, from batch to batch, and from one stem cell line to another. In 2019 Silvia Velasco, Paola Arlotta and colleagues showed that a dorsal forebrain protocol could produce organoids with nearly the same set of cell types as one another, about as consistent as individual brains. In 2020 Aparna Bhaduri, Arnold Kriegstein and colleagues found that cortical organoids show signs of cellular stress and fail to specify neuron subtypes as precisely as the developing brain does.

    Variation matters most when designing an experiment. Organoids from the same batch share their differentiation run and resemble each other. If measurements within a batch are correlated with intraclass correlation \(\rho\), then \(m\) organoids from one batch carry the information of only

    \[ m_{\text{eff}} = \frac{m}{1 + (m-1)\,\rho} \]

    independent ones. As \(m\) grows, this approaches \(1/\rho\): a batch never counts for more than that, however many organoids are imaged. The ratio \(1 + (m-1)\rho\) is the design effect of survey statistics.

    2017 Chapter 8 · Beyond one organoid

    Assembloids, chips, transplants and embryo models

    An organoid lacks most of what surrounds an organ: blood vessels, immune cells, nerves, mechanical forces, and the other organs it talks to. Much current work tries to add them back.

    Assembloids join organoids of different regions. In 2017 Fikri Birey, Sergiu Pașca and colleagues at Stanford fused an organoid of the dorsal forebrain with one of the ventral forebrain. Inhibitory interneurons migrated from the ventral part into the dorsal part, as they do in the fetal brain, and the migration was abnormal in cells from patients with Timothy syndrome. Later assembloids connected cortex to spinal cord and muscle, forming a circuit in which stimulating cortical neurons made muscle contract.

    Organs-on-chips grow cells in microfluidic channels where fluid flows and tissue can be stretched. In 2010 Dongeun Huh, Donald Ingber and colleagues at Harvard's Wyss Institute built a lung-on-a-chip: lung epithelium and capillary endothelium on either side of a porous membrane, stretched rhythmically to imitate breathing. Chips and organoids are now often combined, with organoid-derived cells seeded in perfused channels.

    Transplantation provides vessels and an environment that no dish can. In 2022 Omer Revah, Sergiu Pașca and colleagues transplanted human cortical organoids into the brains of newborn rats. The human neurons grew larger and more mature than in a dish, received input from the rat's sensory pathways, and could influence the rat's reward-seeking behavior.

    Embryo models push self-organization furthest. In 2018 Nicolas Rivron and colleagues assembled mouse stem cells into blastoids, structures resembling the early embryo before implantation, and in 2021 several groups made human blastoids. These raise ethical questions that tissue organoids do not. The International Society for Stem Cell Research revised its guidelines in 2021 to place embryo models under specialized oversight and to prohibit transferring them into a uterus. Brain organoids raise a different question, whether a sufficiently complex one could have any form of experience; current organoids lack the inputs, scale and organization that most researchers think this would require, but bioethicists have asked the field to watch for it.

    Chapter 9 · Now

    Choosing a model

    In December 2022 the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 removed the United States' statutory requirement that new drugs be tested in animals before human trials, allowing cell-based assays and computer models in their place. On 10 April 2025 the FDA published a roadmap to reduce animal testing, starting with monoclonal antibodies, and named organoids and organs-on-chips among the human-based methods it would accept. The agency has not set out which organoid assays will count for which decisions, and validation remains the bottleneck: each assay must show that it predicts human results at least as well as the animal test it replaces.

    The short version

    An organoid is the smallest system that has a human genome, more than one cell type, and the tissue's own architecture. Choose it when those three matter to the question. Account for its limits: no blood supply, a size capped by diffusion, a fate set partly by chance, and variation that makes batches, not organoids, the unit of replication.

    Glossary

    Assembloid
    Two or more organoids of different regions or tissues fused so their cells interact.
    Basement membrane extract
    A gel of laminin and type IV collagen, such as Matrigel, used to embed organoids.
    Blastoid
    A stem-cell-derived model of the blastocyst, the embryo before implantation.
    CFTR
    The chloride channel mutated in cystic fibrosis.
    Crypt
    A pocket in the intestinal lining holding stem cells and Paneth cells.
    Design effect
    The factor by which clustering of measurements inflates the variance of an estimate.
    Differential adhesion hypothesis
    Steinberg's proposal that cells sort like immiscible liquids according to their adhesive strengths.
    Forskolin-induced swelling
    An assay in which CFTR activity drives fluid into organoid lumens, measured as an increase in area.
    Lgr5
    A receptor for R-spondin that marks adult stem cells in the intestine and other epithelia.
    Morphogen
    A signaling molecule whose concentration tells cells their position.
    Neutral drift
    Random replacement among equivalent stem cells that ends with one clone filling the niche.
    Paneth cell
    A secretory cell at the base of the crypt that provides niche signals to stem cells.
    R-spondin
    A secreted protein that amplifies Wnt signaling by binding Lgr5.
