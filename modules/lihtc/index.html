    Prologue

    A subsidy you can sell

    Building an apartment costs about the same whether its tenants earn $30,000 a year or $150,000. Rents set for a low-income household cannot repay the loan that a full-cost building needs. Somebody has to cover the difference. Since 1986, the main way the United States has covered it is a tax credit.

    The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC, said "lie-tech", and often just "the housing credit") is defined in Section 42 of the Internal Revenue Code. It works through a chain of hand-offs:

    1. Each year the federal government gives every state the authority to award a fixed amount of tax credits, based on population.
    2. A state housing agency awards those credits to specific apartment projects through a competition.
    3. The developer cannot use tax credits itself, so it sells them, in effect, to investors (mostly banks), who pay cash up front in exchange for ten years of credits against their federal taxes.
    4. That cash is equity. It replaces debt, so the building needs less mortgage and can charge lower rents.
    5. In return, the owner signs a recorded agreement that keeps the units rent- and income-restricted for at least 30 years.

    The figure at the top shows the result for a typical example. Tax credit equity pays for most of the building. The rents can support only a modest loan. A gap remains, filled by a patchwork of state and local money. That shape, and the many parties needed to assemble it, is what this volume explains.

    Most new affordable rental housing in the United States now involves the credit. Unlike public housing, it is privately owned. Unlike housing vouchers, it subsidizes buildings rather than households. And unlike most federal spending, it never appears as an appropriation: its cost shows up as forgone tax revenue, which the Joint Committee on Taxation estimates at $13.7 billion for 2025 and $14.4 billion for 2026.

    AMI
    Area median income, published annually by HUD for each metro area and rural county. LIHTC limits are percentages of it.
    Credit
    A dollar-for-dollar reduction in federal income tax owed. Unlike a deduction, its value does not depend on the tax rate.
    HFA
    Housing finance agency: the state body that allocates credits and monitors compliance.
    QAP
    Qualified Allocation Plan: the state's published rulebook for scoring and awarding credits.
    1937 Part I · History · Chapter 1

    Fifty years of trying to build it directly

    The federal government's first answer to housing for poor households was to own the buildings. The Housing Act of 1937 created public housing: federally funded, owned and run by local public housing authorities. The Housing Act of 1949 set the goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family."

    From the 1960s, policy shifted toward paying private owners. Section 221(d)(3) (1961) offered below-market-rate mortgages for moderate-income rentals. Section 236 (1968) subsidized mortgage interest down to an effective 1%. Construction boomed, then costs and defaults mounted. In January 1973 the Nixon administration declared a moratorium on new subsidized housing commitments.

    The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 created Section 8, which paid private landlords the difference between a tenant's contribution and a fair market rent. Its new construction component produced hundreds of thousands of units with long-term rental assistance contracts, but it was expensive, and Congress ended new construction under it in 1983.

    The tax shelter era

    Alongside these programs ran a less visible subsidy. Real estate losses could offset other income, and the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 let rental buildings be depreciated over just 15 years. Syndicators packaged apartment projects into limited partnerships and sold interests to high-income individuals, who valued them less for the rent than for the paper losses. Subsidized housing, with its thin cash flow, was a natural host for shelters.

    By the mid-1980s, shelters were a political liability. Efficient for investors, they were an untargeted way to pay for housing, and much of the benefit went to people with no connection to it.

    1986 Chapter 2 · The Tax Reform Act

    A credit born from closing shelters

    The Tax Reform Act of 1986, signed in October of that year, was the largest overhaul of the income tax in decades. It cut rates, broadened the base, and dismantled the shelter industry. New "passive activity" rules stopped most individuals from using rental losses against wages, and depreciation for residential buildings stretched to 27.5 years.

    Without shelters, private capital for low-income rental housing would largely disappear. The new Section 42 was the replacement: a targeted credit, limited in amount, tied to explicit income and rent restrictions, and allocated by states rather than claimed by anyone who built.

    As enacted, the credit was experimental. It was set to expire after 1989. Each state received $1.25 per resident per year in credit authority. Projects had to keep units affordable for a 15-year compliance period. Credits were claimed over ten years, at rates designed to deliver a present value of 70% of construction costs for new buildings or 30% for acquisitions and federally subsidized projects. Those "70% and 30%" targets are still the backbone of the program.

    The early market was thin. The investors were mostly individuals buying through public funds, and the passive loss rules limited how much credit they could use. Credits sold for well under half their face value in the first years: an investor paid roughly 40 to 45 cents for each dollar of future tax reduction.

    1989 Chapter 3 · 1989–2000

    The rules that made it permanent

    The Revenue Reconciliation Act of 1989 rewrote much of Section 42 into the form it still has. Three changes mattered most.

    • Qualified Allocation Plans. States must award credits under a published plan that sets out selection criteria and gives preference to projects that serve the lowest-income tenants, stay affordable longest, and help revitalize distressed areas.
    • Extended use. Owners must record an agreement keeping units affordable for at least 15 years beyond the compliance period, for a minimum of 30 years in total.
    • No more credit than necessary. The allocating agency must find that the credit awarded is no more than the project needs to be financially feasible. This is §42(m)(2), and it turns every state agency into an underwriter.

    The 1989 changes also created the basis boost for projects in high-cost areas and low-income census tracts, covered in Chapter 8. After several short extensions, the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 made the credit permanent.

    Corporations replace individuals

    Permanence made the credit bankable. Corporations, with large and predictable tax bills, were not subject to the passive loss limits that constrained individuals. Banks in particular had a second reason to invest: the Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 requires them to meet the credit needs of the communities they serve, and regulators count LIHTC investments toward that obligation. As corporate demand rose, prices rose with it.

    The Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000 raised the per-capita allocation for the first time, from $1.25 to $1.50 in 2001 and $1.75 in 2002, and indexed it to inflation after that, along with a minimum allocation for small states.

    2008 Chapter 4 · The financial crisis

    When the buyers disappeared

    A credit is only valuable to someone who owes tax. In 2008 the largest buyers stopped owing it. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which together had accounted for roughly 40% of housing credit investment, went into federal conservatorship in September 2008 and stopped investing. Banks that were losing money had no taxable income to offset. Credits that sold for about 95 cents in early 2007 fetched as little as 60 to 68 cents during the downturn, and in many markets deals simply could not be sold.

    Congress responded twice. The Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 (HERA) temporarily fixed the applicable percentage for new construction at no less than 9%, raised state allocations by 10% for 2008 and 2009, let states give a discretionary basis boost to 9% projects, and let credits offset the alternative minimum tax.

    When that was not enough, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 created two stopgaps. The Tax Credit Assistance Program (TCAP) sent $2.25 billion through the HOME program to stalled credit projects. The Section 1602 exchange program let states swap unused 2008 credits and up to 40% of their 2009 credit authority for cash grants at 85 cents per dollar of credit, which they then passed to projects directly. For a year, the federal government was in effect the investor.

    The episode exposed the credit's structural dependence on corporate tax liability. It also showed how tightly the parties are linked: one missing investor, and the agency's award, the lender's commitment, and the contractor's schedule all stall at once.

    2015 Chapter 5 · 2015 to 2026

    Floors, averaging, and expansion

    Since the crisis, Congress has adjusted the credit repeatedly, mostly by expanding it.

    1. 2015The PATH Act makes the 9% minimum rate permanent for new construction.
    2. 2017The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act cuts the corporate rate from 35% to 21%. The credit itself is unchanged, but investors' depreciation losses become less valuable and some have less tax to offset. Prices fall, in anticipation, from late 2016.
    3. 2018The Consolidated Appropriations Act raises state allocations by 12.5% for 2018 through 2021 and creates income averaging (Chapter 7).
    4. 2020The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 sets a 4% minimum rate for acquisition and bond-financed projects, which had floated near 3%.
    5. 2025The 2025 budget reconciliation law (Public Law 119-21) permanently raises state 9% allocations by 12% beginning in 2026 and lowers the bond financing threshold for 4% deals from 50% to 25%, for buildings placed in service after 2025 whose bonds issued after 2025 finance at least 5% of the basis.

    The last change matters more than it sounds. Four-percent credits are not capped by the per-capita allocation. Any project financed at least half by tax-exempt private activity bonds received them automatically. But bonds are themselves capped by a separate state volume limit, and in the early 2020s many states ran out. Halving the share of bonds required lets the same bond cap support roughly twice as many projects.

    §42(g) Part II · Mechanics · Chapter 7

    Who can live there, and what they pay

    A building qualifies only if enough of its units are rented to low-income households at restricted rents. The owner picks one of three minimum set-asides when the building is placed in service, and the choice cannot be changed:

    • 20-50: at least 20% of units go to households earning no more than 50% of AMI.
    • 40-60: at least 40% of units go to households earning no more than 60% of AMI. This is by far the most common.
    • Income averaging: at least 40% of units are designated at income levels between 20% and 80% of AMI, in 10-point steps, and the designations average no more than 60%.

    Meeting the minimum makes the building eligible. In practice most LIHTC buildings are 100% restricted, because credits are earned only on the low-income units.

    Income limits

    HUD publishes Multifamily Tax Subsidy Project (MTSP) income limits for every county each spring. They start from the four-person limit at 50% of AMI and adjust for household size: a one-person household gets 70% of the four-person figure, two people 80%, three 90%, and each person beyond four adds 8 points. Other percentages are scaled from the 50% figure. (HUD's actual computation includes caps, floors, and hold-harmless rules that keep limits from falling in a project's area.)

    Rent limits

    Rent limits are tied to the unit, not the tenant. The statute assumes 1.5 people per bedroom (one person for a studio) and caps gross rent, meaning rent plus a utility allowance for tenant-paid utilities, at 30% of the income limit for that imputed household:

    \[ \text{max gross rent}_{\,b\text{ BR}} = \frac{0.30 \times \text{income limit}\big(\text{designation},\ \max(1,\ 1.5\,b)\ \text{persons}\big)}{12} \]

    Two consequences surprise people. First, the rent does not depend on the tenant's actual income: a household earning 35% of AMI in a 60% unit pays the 60% rent unless it has other assistance. Second, payments from a Section 8 voucher do not count toward the limit, so an owner can collect more than the LIHTC maximum when a voucher covers the difference.

    Income averaging

    Before 2018, a household earning 65% of AMI could not live in a LIHTC unit at all, and nothing rewarded an owner for serving households at 30% of AMI beyond what state QAPs required. Income averaging lets an owner designate some units above 60% and use the extra rent to support units well below it, as long as the average stays at or under 60%. IRS final regulations issued in 2022 settled how the average is computed and how owners can fix a designation that goes wrong.

    §42(b) Chapter 8 · Basis and applicable percentage

    How much credit a building earns

    The annual credit is the product of two numbers: the building's qualified basis, a measure of what it cost to build the low-income part, and the applicable percentage. The owner claims that amount every year for ten years.

    \[ \text{annual credit} = \underbrace{\text{eligible basis} \times \text{boost} \times \text{applicable fraction}}_{\text{qualified basis}} \times \text{applicable percentage} \]

    Eligible basis is the depreciable cost of the building: construction, architecture and engineering, construction interest, developer fee, and similar costs. It excludes land, permanent loan costs, syndication costs, and reserves, none of which can be depreciated. Federal grants also reduce it.

    The basis boost raises eligible basis to 130% for buildings in a HUD-designated Qualified Census Tract (a tract where at least half of households have incomes under 60% of the area median, or the poverty rate is at least 25%) or a Difficult Development Area (where construction, land, and utility costs are high relative to incomes). Since 2008, states may also designate other 9% projects as needing the boost.

    The applicable fraction is the share of the building that is low-income: the smaller of the share of units and the share of floor space. A building with 80 of 100 units restricted, all of average size, has an applicable fraction of 80%.

    Where "9%" and "4%" come from

    The program's names come from round numbers that were originally approximations. The law aims for ten years of credits worth a present value of 70% of qualified basis (for new construction and substantial rehab without other federal subsidy), or 30% (for acquisition of existing buildings and for bond-financed projects). Treasury computes, each month, the annual rate \(p\) that hits those targets, discounting at 72% of the average applicable federal rate (AFR):

    \[ p \cdot \sum_{t=1}^{10} \frac{1}{(1+d)^t} = 0.70 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \frac{0.70\, d}{1-(1+d)^{-10}}, \qquad d = 0.72 \times \text{AFR} \]

    When rates were high, the result was close to 9% and 4%. As interest rates fell after 2000, the floating rates sank, and with them the credit each project earned. Congress fixed the floors at 9% (temporarily in 2008, permanently in 2015) and 4% (from 2021). Today the floors bind whenever rates are anything like recent norms, so the floating formula mostly matters as history.

    From credits to cash

    The developer turns credits into equity by admitting an investor to the ownership partnership. The investor pays a price per dollar of total credits, usually quoted in cents: at $0.88, ten years of $1.93 million in annual credits ($19.3 million in total) brings in about $17 million. Price depends on investor demand, interest rates, the market, and the deal's risk. It has ranged from around 40 cents in the program's first year to a national average of about $1.05 at the peak of bank demand in late 2016, with some heavily bank-driven markets paying more.

    §42(h) Chapter 9 · State ceilings and the QAP

    Allocation: a competition run by each state

    Nine-percent credits are scarce by design. Each state's annual ceiling equals a per-capita amount times its population, with a minimum for small states. The per-capita figure is indexed to inflation: $3.00 in 2025, with a small-state minimum of $3,455,000. For 2026, after the 12% increase, it is $3.416, with a minimum of $3,953,600. States add credits returned from failed projects and may receive a share of a national pool of unused credits.

    Demand far exceeds supply. In a typical round, applications request well over the available credits, often two or three times as much. Who wins is decided by the state's Qualified Allocation Plan.

    What a QAP contains

    Federal law sets the frame. A QAP must give preference to projects that serve the lowest-income tenants, that stay affordable longest, and that are in qualified census tracts and contribute to a concerted community revitalization plan. It must consider location, housing needs, the use of existing housing, sponsor characteristics, tenant populations with special needs, public housing waiting lists, individuals with children, projects intended for eventual tenant ownership, energy efficiency, and historic character. At least 10% of the ceiling must be set aside for projects with a qualified nonprofit materially participating.

    Within that frame, states differ enormously. QAPs run to more than a hundred pages and are revised every year or two after public comment. Most combine threshold requirements (site control, zoning, financial feasibility, experience) with a point system. Many divide the ceiling into pools by geography or population, such as rural, urban, senior, or supportive housing. Ties are broken by rules as specific as cost per unit or a lottery.

    The 4% path

    Four-percent credits work differently. They are not limited by the state's 9% ceiling. A project gets them automatically if it is financed largely with tax-exempt private activity bonds, which are capped by a separate state bond volume limit. The agency still reviews the project, issues a determination of the credit amount, and enforces the same rent and income rules, but the competition is for bond cap, not credits. Because 4% credits produce less than half the equity of 9% credits for the same building, these deals lean heavily on bond-financed debt, larger soft loans, and often local subsidies.

    Who Part III · Logistics · Chapter 10

    Fourteen parties, one building

    A single LIHTC property can involve a dozen organizations or more, each with its own documents, approvals, and incentives. Understanding the program in practice means understanding these relationships: who pays whom, who carries the credits, who signs what, and who watches whom.

    The central legal fact is the ownership structure. The building is owned by a single-purpose partnership or LLC. The investor is its limited partner with about 99.99% of the interests, so it receives nearly all the credits and depreciation. The developer's affiliate is the general partner with about 0.01% and runs the property. The investor's money comes in as capital contributions; its return comes almost entirely as tax benefits, not cash.

    The documents that bind them

    • Application and reservation. The developer applies under the QAP; the agency's reservation letter promises credits subject to conditions.
    • Carryover allocation. If the building will not be finished in the award year, the agency allocates by December 31 and the owner must spend more than 10% of expected basis within 12 months.
    • Limited partnership agreement. The longest and most negotiated document. It sets the equity price, the installment schedule and conditions for each payment, guarantees from the developer, adjusters if credits come in lower or later than projected, cash flow splits, and the Year 15 exit.
    • Extended use agreement. Also called a land use restriction agreement (LURA) or regulatory agreement. Recorded against the land, it binds all future owners to the rent and income limits for the full term.
    • Loan documents for each lender, each with its own regulatory agreement if public money is involved, and an intercreditor agreement ranking them.
    • Cost certification and Form 8609. After completion, a CPA certifies costs; the agency issues Form 8609 for each building, confirming the credit amount. No 8609, no credits.
    Deal Chapter 11 · From application to Year 30

    The life of a deal

    A 9% project usually takes three to four years from application to Form 8609, after years of site work and predevelopment beforehand. Its legal life runs at least 30 years after that. The calendar is driven by statutory deadlines, and missing one can cost the credits.

    Why equity arrives late

    Investors pay in installments tied to milestones, because each one removes a risk: closing (the deal exists), construction completion (it was built), stabilization (it leased up with qualified tenants and covers its debt), and Form 8609 (the credits are confirmed). Most of the money arrives after construction. So the developer needs a construction loan sized for nearly the full cost, repaid at conversion by the later equity installments and the permanent loan. A bridge loan against future equity is common.

    The developer fee

    QAPs typically cap developer fees at a percentage of costs, often around 10 to 15%. The fee is included in eligible basis, so it generates credits. Part of it is usually deferred, paid from the property's cash flow over the following years, which lets the fee double as a source that fills the gap. A deferred fee that cannot plausibly be repaid within the compliance period may be excluded from basis, so agencies and investors scrutinize it.

    Equity Chapter 12 · Why a bank buys credits

    The investor's side of the ledger

    An investor in a LIHTC partnership expects to get back little or no cash. Its return is the credits, plus the tax value of its share of the property's depreciation and interest losses. Because the credits are fixed dollar amounts that arrive on a known schedule, investors treat them much like a bond, and price them to a modest after-tax yield.

    The return is sensitive to everything that determines the timing and amount of tax benefits: the price paid, when the equity is paid in, how fast the building leases up, and the investor's tax rate, which affects how much the losses are worth but not the credits themselves.

    How investors protect themselves

    The partnership agreement shifts most risks to the developer. Common protections include a construction completion guarantee; an operating deficit guarantee that funds shortfalls for some years after stabilization, usually capped; a credit adjuster that reduces later equity installments if the credits delivered fall short of projections; and, in serious cases, a repurchase obligation. Reserves held by the partnership (operating, replacement, and sometimes lease-up reserves) back these promises.

    Investors also care about recapture. If a building falls out of compliance during the 15-year compliance period, part of the credits already claimed must be paid back with interest (Chapter 13). Losses from recapture have been rare: industry surveys have long reported very low foreclosure rates on LIHTC properties, which is part of why banks like the asset.

    Most investors buy through a syndicator's fund, which spreads risk across dozens of properties and handles 15 years of asset management for a fee. Large banks often invest directly or through proprietary funds built for a single investor.

    §42(j) Chapter 13 · Compliance

    Fifteen years of paperwork

    Credits are earned unit by unit, month by month. If a unit is rented to an over-income household, charges too much rent, or fails inspection, the building's qualified basis can fall, and so do the credits. Most of the program's day-to-day work happens in a leasing office.

    What the property must do

    • Certify every tenant's income before move-in, with third-party verification of wages, benefits, and assets, documented on a tenant income certification.
    • Keep rents, including the utility allowance, at or below the limit for each unit's designation, and update utility allowances annually.
    • Apply the next available unit rule. A tenant whose income rises above 140% of the limit may stay, and the unit still counts, but the next comparable unit that becomes vacant must go to a qualified household.
    • Keep units suitable for occupancy, meeting local codes and the agency's physical inspection standards.
    • File annual owner certifications with the agency and keep records for the life of the deal.

    What the agency must do

    Treasury regulations require state agencies to monitor compliance. Agencies review the owner's annual certifications. At least once every three years they inspect every building in each project, and for at least 20% of its low-income units, chosen at random without advance notice, inspect the units and review the tenant files and rent records. They report any noncompliance to the IRS on Form 8823, even if the owner corrects it. Most states charge monitoring fees to pay for this, and many keep monitoring through the extended use period under the recorded agreement.

    Recapture

    If qualified basis decreases during the 15-year compliance period, the owner must repay the accelerated portion of the credits already taken, plus interest. Because credits are claimed over 10 years but the building owes 15 years of compliance, the accelerated portion of a full ten years' credits is one third:

    \[ \text{accelerated portion} = \text{credits claimed} - \frac{10}{15}\times\text{credits claimed} = \tfrac{1}{3}\times\text{credits claimed} \]

    In practice the threat of recapture is what keeps owners, investors, and managers attentive. Before 2008, a seller during the compliance period had to post a bond against recapture. Since HERA, a sale triggers no recapture as long as it is reasonably expected that the building will keep complying.

    Year 15 Chapter 14 · §42(i)(7) and §42(h)(6)

    The exit, and what comes after

    After 15 years the investor has received its credits and most of its losses, and no longer has a reason to stay. The partnership agreement anticipates this. Usually the general partner or an affiliated nonprofit buys the investor's interest for a modest price, often just enough to cover the investor's exit taxes, and the building continues under the extended use agreement.

    Section 42(i)(7) helps. It allows a nonprofit, tenant organization, or government agency to hold a right of first refusal to buy the property after the compliance period at a minimum price: the outstanding debt plus the investor's exit taxes. The price can be far below market value.

    Disputes at exit

    In the 2010s, some investor limited partners, and firms that had bought investor interests in older funds, contested nonprofits' rights of first refusal. They argued that, as with a conventional right of first refusal, it could be exercised only after the partnership received and agreed to accept a bona fide third-party offer. Litigation followed. In Homeowner's Rehab, Inc. v. Related Corporate V SLP (2018), the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held, on the terms of that partnership's agreements, that the nonprofit could exercise its right without such an offer. Several states and many new partnership agreements now spell the terms out more precisely.

    The qualified contract

    For buildings whose owners did not waive it, the 1989 law created a release valve. After year 14, the owner can ask the agency to find a buyer willing to pay a formula price, roughly the outstanding debt plus the investors' adjusted equity. If no buyer is found within a year, the extended use restrictions end, subject to a three-year protection period for existing tenants. Because the formula price often exceeds what an affordable property is worth, many states now require owners to waive the option in exchange for credits.

    Preservation

    Properties from the program's first decade have now passed 30 years. Some convert to market rate when restrictions expire, especially in strong markets. Many others are recapitalized: acquired by a new partnership, rehabilitated with 4% credits and bonds, and given a new affordability term. The aging of the stock makes preservation a growing share of the program's work.

    Now Chapter 15 · The arguments

    What the credit does well, and what it doesn't

    The credit has broad bipartisan support, partly because it uses private capital, puts states in charge, and has a low rate of financial failure. Researchers and practitioners still argue about several of its features.

    • Cost. Development costs per unit often exceed those of comparable market-rate buildings, driven by layered financing, prevailing wage and design requirements, and transaction costs. A 2018 GAO review found median new-construction costs ranging from about $126,000 per unit in Texas to about $326,000 in California, and limited federal oversight of costs. It recommended better collection and analysis of cost data.
    • Efficiency. Because the subsidy passes through investors, syndicators, and developers, less than a dollar of equity reaches the building for each dollar of forgone revenue. Supporters answer that those intermediaries also underwrite, monitor, and guarantee performance, services a direct grant would have to pay for some other way.
    • Depth. Rents set at 60% of AMI are not affordable to households at 30% of AMI. Research using state tenant data (O'Regan and Horn, 2013) found that a large share of LIHTC tenants have extremely low incomes and that most of them also receive rental assistance. Without vouchers, the credit mostly serves households just below the limits.
    • Location. The statute rewards building in low-income census tracts, which revitalizes neighborhoods but can concentrate poverty. Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project (2015), in which the Supreme Court held that disparate-impact claims can be brought under the Fair Housing Act, began as a challenge to how Texas allocated credits. Many QAPs now give points for high-opportunity areas, and the tension between the two goals is unresolved.
    • Neighborhood effects and crowd-out. Studies have reached different conclusions about how much LIHTC construction adds to the total housing stock rather than displacing private building (Eriksen and Rosenthal, 2010; Baum-Snow and Marion, 2009). Diamond and McQuade (2019) found that new LIHTC developments raised nearby property values and lowered crime in low-income neighborhoods.
    • Permanence. A 30-year commitment is long for a tax incentive and short for a building. Every year, more units approach the end of their restrictions.
    The core trade-off

    The credit is a production subsidy: it lowers the cost of building units and caps their rents. Vouchers are a demand subsidy: they close the gap between rent and what a household can pay. Most analysts see them as complements. The credit builds the building; the voucher makes it affordable to the poorest tenants.
