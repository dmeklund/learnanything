    Self and Nonself

    How the immune system tells the body's own cells from invaders: the fast, inherited defenses of innate immunity, the antibodies and T cells of adaptive immunity, the gene shuffling that builds billions of different receptors, the education that keeps them from attacking the self, and the memory that vaccines exploit.

    Prologue

    Two ways to recognize an enemy

    Every surface of the body is under constant attack by bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, which multiply far faster than human cells. The immune system has two strategies for dealing with them.

    Innate immunity is inherited and fast. Its receptors, encoded directly in the genome, recognize molecular patterns shared by whole classes of microbes, such as the cell walls of bacteria or the double-stranded RNA of many viruses. Within minutes to hours, cells like neutrophils and macrophages arrive and engulf the invaders.

    Adaptive immunity is learned and slow at first, but exquisitely specific. Each B or T lymphocyte carries a receptor assembled by randomly shuffling gene segments, so the body holds billions of different receptors, almost any of which might fit some future invader. When one does, that cell multiplies into an army of identical clones, and some of them remain as memory. This is why most people catch measles only once, and why vaccines work.

    The figure above shows that idea in miniature: a repertoire of clones, each recognizing a slightly different shape, and an antigen that activates only the few that fit. The central puzzle, which took most of the twentieth century to solve, is how such a random system avoids attacking the body itself.

    Part I

    Recognition

    1882 Chapter 1 · Innate immunity

    A thorn in a starfish larva

    In December 1882 the Russian zoologist Élie Metchnikoff, working in Messina, pushed rose thorns into the transparent larvae of starfish. The next morning the thorns were surrounded by mobile cells trying to engulf them. Metchnikoff called them phagocytes, "eating cells," and argued that they were the body's main defense against microbes. He shared the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Paul Ehrlich, whose rival theory emphasized antibodies in the blood. Both were right.

    For most of the next century, innate immunity was treated as crude and nonspecific. In 1989 Charles Janeway argued that it must recognize microbes through pattern recognition receptors that detect molecules shared by microbes and absent from the host, and that this recognition was what switched on adaptive immunity. The receptors were found within a decade. Jules Hoffmann's group showed in 1996 that fruit flies lacking the protein Toll die of fungal infections, and in 1998 Bruce Beutler identified Toll-like receptor 4 as the mammalian sensor of bacterial lipopolysaccharide. Ralph Steinman had discovered dendritic cells in 1973; they carry pieces of microbes from the site of infection to the lymph nodes and present them to T cells. Hoffmann, Beutler, and Steinman shared the 2011 Nobel Prize.

    A race

    An infection is a race between microbial growth and the innate response. A few hundred bacteria can be engulfed by the neutrophils already patrolling a tissue; a larger dose can grow faster than neutrophils arrive, and once the bacteria outnumber the phagocytes, each phagocyte can only eat so many. That saturation creates a threshold dose above which the infection escapes local control.

    1890 Chapter 2 · Antibodies

    Protection in the blood

    In December 1890 Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato, working in Robert Koch's institute in Berlin, showed that serum from animals immunized against diphtheria or tetanus toxin could protect other animals. Something in the blood, which they called antitoxin, neutralized the poison. Serum therapy for diphtheria followed within a few years, and Behring received the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901. In 1897 Ehrlich proposed that cells carry "side chains" that bind toxins specifically and are shed into the blood when overproduced, the first receptor theory.

    The antitoxins are antibodies. In the late 1950s and 1960s Rodney Porter and Gerald Edelman worked out their structure: a Y-shaped molecule of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains. The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ from one antibody to the next and form the binding site; the stem is the constant region, which tells the rest of the immune system what to do with whatever is bound. They shared the 1972 Nobel Prize.

    Affinity and avidity

    An antibody arm binds its target reversibly. At equilibrium, the fraction of target sites occupied depends on the antibody concentration \(c\) and the dissociation constant \(K_d\), the concentration at which half the sites are bound:

    \[ \theta = \frac{c}{c + K_d}, \qquad K_d = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}}. \]

    Antibodies early in a response have \(K_d\) around a micromolar; after affinity maturation (Chapter 3) the best reach the nanomolar to picomolar range. Because an IgG antibody has two identical arms, once one arm is bound to a surface with many copies of the antigen, such as a virus or bacterium, the second arm is held close to another copy and binds easily. The combined grip, called avidity, can be orders of magnitude stronger than either arm alone. IgM, the first antibody class made in a response, has ten arms, which compensates for its lower affinity.

    1957 Chapter 3 · Clonal selection

    Selection, not instruction

    How can the body make an antibody against a chemical that never existed before, such as a synthetic dye? Through the 1930s and 1940s the leading answer was instruction: the antigen acted as a template, and antibodies folded around it. In 1955 Niels Jerne proposed instead that the body already contains antibodies of every specificity and that antigen merely selects among them. In 1957 Macfarlane Burnet sharpened this into the clonal selection theory: each lymphocyte makes antibody of a single specificity; an antigen binds the few cells whose receptors fit; those cells multiply into a clone and secrete that antibody; and clones that recognize the body's own molecules are eliminated early in life.

    In 1958 Gustav Nossal and Joshua Lederberg showed that single antibody-forming cells make antibody of only one specificity, as the theory required. Burnet shared the 1960 Nobel Prize with Peter Medawar, whose experiments on skin grafts had shown that tolerance to foreign tissue could be acquired early in life. In 1975 Georges Köhler and César Milstein fused single antibody-producing cells with tumor cells to make immortal clones secreting one antibody, monoclonal antibodies; they shared the 1984 Nobel Prize with Jerne.

    Affinity maturation

    The response improves as it goes. Activated B cells enter germinal centers in lymph nodes, where they divide rapidly and an enzyme, activation-induced cytidine deaminase (identified by Tasuku Honjo's group in 2000), introduces mutations into their antibody genes at roughly a million times the normal rate. The mutated cells then compete for antigen and for help from T cells; those with higher affinity win and divide again. Over weeks, this Darwinian cycle of mutation and selection raises antibody affinity a hundredfold or more.

    1976 Chapter 4 · Gene rearrangement

    Billions of receptors from a few hundred genes

    Clonal selection raised a new puzzle. The human genome has about 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet the immune system makes billions of different antibodies. In 1976 Nobumichi Hozumi and Susumu Tonegawa, at the Basel Institute for Immunology, compared antibody genes in embryonic cells and in antibody-producing tumor cells. In the embryo, the pieces encoding the variable region lay far apart; in the antibody-making cell, they had been joined. Antibody genes are assembled by cutting and pasting DNA. Tonegawa received the 1987 Nobel Prize.

    Each heavy-chain variable region is built from one of about 40 functional V segments, one of 23 D segments, and one of 6 J segments; light chains use V and J only. The enzymes RAG1 and RAG2, identified by David Schatz, Marjorie Oettinger, and David Baltimore in 1989 and 1990, cut the DNA at the segment boundaries. Diversity multiplies at three levels:

    • Combinatorial: the choice of segments, times the choice of pairing between a heavy and a light chain.
    • Junctional: the joints are imprecise. Nucleotides are trimmed and random nucleotides added by the enzyme TdT, so the most variable part of the receptor, the third complementarity-determining region, differs even between cells that chose the same segments.
    • Somatic hypermutation: after activation, B cells keep mutating (Chapter 3). T cell receptors, assembled the same way from their own segment families, do not.

    The potential diversity far exceeds the number of lymphocytes in a body, so each person carries only a sample of the possible repertoire.

    1974 Chapter 5 · T cells

    Recognizing fragments on a platter

    Antibodies recognize intact molecules. T cells do something stranger. In 1974 Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty, in Canberra, found that mouse T cells killed virus-infected cells only if the infected cells carried the same version of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) as the mouse the T cells came from. T cells recognize not the virus but fragments of viral protein displayed on MHC molecules, the body's own platters. They shared the 1996 Nobel Prize.

    MHC class I molecules, on nearly every cell, display peptides from proteins made inside that cell, so a cell infected by a virus displays viral peptides to cytotoxic (CD8) T cells, which kill it. MHC class II molecules, on specialized antigen-presenting cells, display peptides from material the cell has engulfed to helper (CD4) T cells, which coordinate the response. The genes for the T cell receptor were cloned in 1984 by Mark Davis and Stephen Hedrick and by Tak Mak's group.

    How a T cell tells a foreign peptide from a self peptide

    A T cell faces a needle-in-a-haystack problem: a few foreign peptide–MHC complexes among tens of thousands of self complexes on the same cell, some binding the receptor only a little less tightly. Equilibrium binding cannot discriminate that well. In 1995 Timothy McKeithan proposed kinetic proofreading, an idea John Hopfield had introduced in 1974 for the accuracy of protein synthesis. After the receptor binds, it must complete a series of \(N\) modification steps, each at rate \(k_p\), before it signals; if the peptide falls off first, everything resets. The chance of completing the chain is

    \[ P = \left(\frac{k_p}{k_p + k_{\text{off}}}\right)^{N}, \]

    so a tenfold difference in how long a peptide stays bound becomes a difference of about \(10^N\) in signaling. Delay buys specificity.

    Part II

    Tolerance, memory, and medicine

    1995 Chapter 6 · Tolerance

    Learning not to attack the self

    A repertoire built at random will contain receptors that recognize the body's own molecules. Ehrlich called the danger horror autotoxicus. The first defense is education in the thymus, a gland above the heart where T cells mature.

    Developing T cells, thymocytes, are tested against self peptides displayed on MHC. Those whose receptors cannot engage self MHC at all are useless and die by neglect. Those that engage it weakly receive a survival signal: positive selection. Those that bind self peptide–MHC too strongly are potential autoimmune attackers and are killed: negative selection. Only about 2 to 5% of thymocytes survive. A transcription regulator called AIRE, described in 2002, lets specialized thymic cells express thousands of proteins normally made only in other organs, such as insulin, so that T cells reactive to them can be deleted before they leave.

    Regulatory T cells

    Deletion is incomplete. In 1995 Shimon Sakaguchi identified a population of CD4 T cells carrying the marker CD25 whose removal caused widespread autoimmune disease in mice: regulatory T cells, which actively suppress other T cells. In 2001 Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell found that the "scurfy" mice, which die of runaway autoimmunity, carry mutations in a gene they named Foxp3, and that mutations in the human version cause the fatal disease IPEX. In 2003 Sakaguchi and others showed that Foxp3 controls the development of regulatory T cells. Brunkow, Ramsdell, and Sakaguchi shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries concerning peripheral immune tolerance."

    When tolerance fails, the result is autoimmune disease: type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and dozens of others, together affecting several percent of people.

    1796 Chapter 7 · Memory and vaccines

    Immunity on purpose

    Immunological memory was exploited long before it was understood. People who survived smallpox never caught it again, and in parts of Asia and Africa healthy people were deliberately infected with material from mild cases, variolation, which Lady Mary Wortley Montagu popularized in England from 1721. On 14 May 1796 Edward Jenner inoculated a boy, James Phipps, with material from a cowpox sore on a milkmaid's hand, and later showed that Phipps resisted smallpox. Jenner called the procedure vaccination, from vacca, cow. Louis Pasteur generalized it with weakened pathogens, for chicken cholera around 1880 and rabies in 1885. Vaccination eradicated smallpox, declared by the World Health Assembly on 8 May 1980.

    Primary and secondary responses

    The first encounter with an antigen starts from a handful of naive cells, so antibody appears only after a lag of about a week and peaks after one to two weeks. Some of the expanded B and T cells remain as long-lived memory cells. A second encounter starts from that larger, already affinity-matured pool, so the response comes within days, climbs higher, and produces better antibodies. Booster doses of vaccines exploit exactly this.

    Herd immunity

    Vaccines protect people who are not vaccinated too. An infection with basic reproduction number \(R_0\), the average number of people one case infects in a fully susceptible population, cannot spread once fewer than \(1/R_0\) of contacts are susceptible. If a vaccine prevents infection with efficacy \(E\), the share of people who must be vaccinated is

    \[ p_c = \frac{1 - 1/R_0}{E}. \]

    For measles, with \(R_0\) commonly estimated at 12 to 18, the threshold is above 90%, which is why measles returns quickly when vaccination rates slip.

    Chapter 8 · Today

    Engineering the immune system

    The discoveries of this volume have become a large share of modern medicine.

    • Monoclonal antibodies. From Köhler and Milstein's hybridomas came antibody drugs that block molecules in inflammation, cancer, and infection; they are among the best-selling medicines in the world.
    • Checkpoint inhibitors. T cells carry brakes, such as CTLA-4 and PD-1, that keep responses in check. James Allison and Tasuku Honjo showed that blocking them unleashes T cells against tumors; the first such drug, ipilimumab, was approved in 2011, and they shared the 2018 Nobel Prize. Releasing the brakes can also cause autoimmunity, the flip side of the tolerance mechanisms of Chapter 6.
    • CAR T cells. A patient's own T cells are engineered to carry a chimeric antigen receptor that recognizes a tumor protein. The first was approved by the FDA in 2017 for a childhood leukemia.
    • mRNA vaccines. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found in 2005 that modifying the nucleosides of synthetic mRNA lets it avoid the innate immune sensors of Chapter 1. The result made the COVID-19 vaccines possible, and they shared the 2023 Nobel Prize.
    • Regulatory T cell therapies. The newest direction reverses the others: expanding or engineering regulatory T cells to calm autoimmune disease and prevent transplant rejection.

    The common thread

    The adaptive immune system is evolution run inside one body: variation by random gene assembly and mutation, selection by antigen, and amplification of the winners. Everything else in this volume, from thymic education to regulatory T cells, exists to keep that powerful random generator from turning on the self.

    Glossary

    Affinity
    The strength of binding between one binding site and its target, measured by the dissociation constant.
    Avidity
    The combined binding strength of several binding sites acting together.
    Clonal selection
    The theory that antigen activates the pre-existing lymphocytes whose receptors fit it, which then multiply.
    Germinal center
    A site in lymphoid tissue where activated B cells mutate and are selected for higher affinity.
    Kinetic proofreading
    A mechanism in which a series of delays after binding amplifies differences in binding lifetime.
    MHC
    Major histocompatibility complex: molecules that display peptide fragments to T cells.
    Pattern recognition receptor
    An innate receptor, such as a Toll-like receptor, that detects molecules common to microbes.
    Phagocyte
    A cell that engulfs and digests particles, such as a neutrophil or macrophage.
    R₀
    The basic reproduction number: average new infections caused by one case in a fully susceptible population.
    Regulatory T cell
    A T cell, defined by the transcription factor Foxp3, that suppresses other immune responses.
    Somatic hypermutation
    Rapid mutation of antibody genes in activated B cells.
    V(D)J recombination
    Assembly of receptor genes by joining variable, diversity, and joining gene segments.
