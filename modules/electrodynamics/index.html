    Prologue

    Four equations and the speed of light

    For two thousand years electricity and magnetism were curiosities: rubbed amber that picks up straw, lodestones that point north. Between 1785 and 1865 they became one subject, described by a handful of equations that James Clerk Maxwell assembled and Oliver Heaviside wrote in the compact form used today:

    \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \]Gauss: charges are sources of electric field
    \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0 \]no magnetic charges; field lines close on themselves
    \[ \nabla\times\mathbf E = -\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \]Faraday: a changing magnetic field drives an electric field
    \[ \nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \]Ampère, with Maxwell's addition

    Together with the Lorentz force \(\mathbf F = q(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\), which says how the fields push on charges, these equations describe every electrical and magnetic phenomenon short of the quantum scale: motors and generators, radio and radar, the optics of lenses and fibers, and the glow of a hot filament. Their most surprising prediction was that disturbances in the fields travel as waves at a speed fixed by two constants measured on the bench,

    \[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} \approx 3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}, \]

    which is the speed of light. This volume follows the experiments that led to each equation, then the consequences: waves, antennas, moving charges, and the conflict with Newtonian mechanics that Einstein resolved in 1905.

    Part I

    Statics and induction

    1785 Chapter 1 · Electrostatics

    Charges, lines, and flux

    In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, a French military engineer, hung a light needle from a fine silver wire and measured how far the wire twisted when a charged ball repelled the needle's charged tip. The force fell as the inverse square of the distance, like Newton's gravity:

    \[ F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}. \]

    Joseph Priestley had inferred the inverse square in 1767, and Henry Cavendish had measured it about 1773 without publishing, but Coulomb's torsion balance made it quantitative. Unlike gravity, the force can attract or repel, because charges come in two signs.

    Michael Faraday, who had little mathematics, pictured the force differently. Around every charge he imagined lines of force running from positive charges to negative ones, crowded where the force is strong. The picture became physics when it turned out that the lines behave like something real: they can be counted. Carl Friedrich Gauss showed in 1835 (published in 1867) that the total flux of the electric field out of any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it:

    \[ \oint_S \mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_{\text{inside}}}{\varepsilon_0}. \]

    Every line that enters a surface from an outside charge also leaves it, so outside charges contribute nothing. Lines that start inside must cross the surface, whatever its shape. This is the first of Maxwell's equations in integral form, and it is how the fields of spheres, wires, and plates are computed in a few lines.

    1820 Chapter 2 · Magnetostatics

    A compass needle beside a wire

    In April 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted, lecturing in Copenhagen, noticed that a compass needle moved when current from a battery flowed through a nearby wire. He published in July. Within months the news had spread across Europe. In Paris, Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart measured how the magnetic field of a wire falls with distance, and André-Marie Ampère showed that two parallel wires carrying currents attract or repel each other, without any magnet at all. Magnetism, it seemed, was electricity in motion.

    The Biot–Savart law gives the field of any current distribution as a sum of contributions from each short piece of wire,

    \[ d\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}, \]

    and Ampère's law expresses the same physics as a statement about loops: the field summed around any closed path equals \(\mu_0\) times the current threading it, \(\oint \mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \mu_0 I\). Magnetic field lines never start or stop; they close on themselves, which is the content of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0\).

    A uniform field from two coils

    A single circular loop makes a field that is strongest at its center and falls off along the axis. Two identical coils sharing an axis do better. When their separation equals their radius, the arrangement named after Hermann von Helmholtz, the falloffs of the two fields cancel to high order at the midpoint: the first three derivatives of the field along the axis vanish there. Helmholtz coils are still the standard way to make a uniform, known magnetic field, for calibrating magnetometers or cancelling Earth's field around a sensitive experiment.

    1831 Chapter 3 · Induction

    Changing fields make currents

    If currents make magnetism, can magnetism make currents? Faraday spent years looking. On 29 August 1831, at the Royal Institution in London, he wound two separate coils of wire on an iron ring, connected one to a battery and the other to a galvanometer, and saw the needle jump at the moment he closed the battery circuit and jump the other way when he opened it. A steady current in one coil did nothing; only a changing one induced current in the other. Joseph Henry found the same effect independently in America.

    The rule, in modern form, is that the voltage induced around a loop, its electromotive force, equals the rate of change of magnetic flux through it,

    \[ \mathcal E = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \Phi_B = \int \mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A. \]

    The minus sign is Lenz's law (1834): the induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change. Every electrical generator, transformer, and induction cooktop runs on this equation. In differential form it is the third of Maxwell's equations, \(\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial\mathbf B/\partial t\), and it says something stranger than the circuit version: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field even where there is no wire at all.

    Part II

    Waves

    1865 Chapter 4 · Maxwell

    Light is an electromagnetic wave

    James Clerk Maxwell, working first at King's College London, set out to put Faraday's lines of force into mathematics. In "On Physical Lines of Force" (1861–62) he found that Ampère's law, as it stood, was inconsistent with the conservation of charge when currents change. In a charging capacitor, current flows in the wires but not across the gap. He added a term, the displacement current \(\varepsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t\), which acts like a current wherever the electric field changes.

    With that term, the equations admit waves. In empty space, taking the curl of Faraday's law and using Ampère's gives

    \[ \nabla^2\mathbf E = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}, \]

    a wave equation with speed \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\). In 1856 Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch had measured the ratio of electrical units by discharging a capacitor, and Maxwell computed from their data a speed of 310,740 km/s. Hippolyte Fizeau had measured the speed of light in 1849 as about 315,000 km/s. Maxwell wrote that "we can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena." His "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field" (1865) presented the complete theory.

    Waves in matter

    In a material such as glass, the electric field polarizes the atoms, which raises the effective permittivity to \(\varepsilon = n^2\varepsilon_0\) and slows the wave to \(c/n\), where \(n\) is the refractive index. Where the index changes abruptly, part of the wave reflects. For light arriving straight on, the fraction of energy reflected is given by Augustin-Jean Fresnel's formula, \(R = \big((n-1)/(n+1)\big)^2\), about 4% for glass. A thin coating of intermediate index, a quarter-wavelength thick, makes the reflections from its two surfaces cancel. This is the anti-reflection coating on camera lenses and eyeglasses.

    The figure solves Maxwell's equations directly on a grid with the method Kane Yee introduced in 1966, now called finite-difference time-domain (FDTD). Electric and magnetic fields are stored at alternating points in space and updated in alternating half-steps of time, each from the curl of the other.

    1887 Chapter 5 · Radiation

    Sparks across a room

    Maxwell died in 1879 without seeing his waves produced. In 1887, at the technical university in Karlsruhe, Heinrich Hertz drove a spark gap between two metal rods with an induction coil, making charge slosh back and forth hundreds of millions of times a second. A loop of wire with a tiny gap, across the room, sparked in sympathy. Hertz measured the waves' wavelength from standing-wave patterns against a reflecting wall, showed they could be reflected, refracted, and polarized like light, and found their speed equal to light's. "It's of no use whatsoever," he reportedly said of the discovery.

    An oscillating charge radiates because its field cannot keep up with it, as Chapter 7 shows. For a short antenna, the radiated power goes mostly sideways, perpendicular to the antenna, and none along its axis. Within fifteen years Guglielmo Marconi had sent signals across the Atlantic (1901).

    Steering a beam with phase

    Several antennas fed from the same source make a pattern that depends on their spacing \(d\) and the phase difference \(\delta\) between neighbors. Waves from all \(N\) elements add in directions where their path differences, \(d\cos\theta\) per element, compensate the phase steps:

    \[ P(\theta) \propto \left|\sum_{m=0}^{N-1} e^{\,i m\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\cos\theta + \delta\right)}\right|^2. \]

    Changing \(\delta\) electronically swings the beam without moving anything. Ferdinand Braun demonstrated directional transmission with phased antennas in 1905 and shared the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics with Marconi. Today's radars, 5G base stations, and satellite internet terminals steer beams this way. If the elements are more than half a wavelength apart, extra beams called grating lobes appear, the antenna version of the orders of a diffraction grating.

    Part III

    Charges in motion

    1897 Chapter 6 · The Lorentz force

    Bending a beam of particles

    Maxwell's equations say how charges make fields. The other half, how fields push charges, was written out in full by Hendrik Lorentz in 1895:

    \[ \mathbf F = q\left(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B\right). \]

    The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes a particle's direction but never its speed. In a uniform magnetic field a charged particle circles with radius \(r = mv/qB\) and a period \(2\pi m/qB\) that does not depend on its speed, the fact that makes cyclotrons work. Add an electric field perpendicular to the magnetic one and the circle drifts sideways at the speed \(E/B\), in the direction of \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\), whatever the particle's charge.

    In 1897 J. J. Thomson at the Cavendish Laboratory sent cathode rays through crossed electric and magnetic fields. He adjusted them until the beam went straight, which meant \(qE = qvB\) and gave the particles' speed \(v = E/B\); bending the beam with the magnetic field alone then gave the ratio of charge to mass. It was about a thousand times larger than for hydrogen ions. The rays were made of a new, very light particle, soon called the electron. The same crossed-field drift confines plasma in fusion devices and sorts particles in mass spectrometers.

    1905 Chapter 7 · Relativity and radiation

    Why accelerating charges radiate

    Maxwell's equations contain a fixed speed, \(c\). In Newtonian mechanics, speeds add, so the equations should look different to observers moving relative to each other; a magnet moving past a wire and a wire moving past a magnet would be different phenomena. They are not: the induced current is the same either way. In 1905 Albert Einstein began "On the electrodynamics of moving bodies" with exactly this example. He resolved the conflict by keeping Maxwell's equations and changing mechanics: space and time transform so that the speed of light is the same for every observer. Electric and magnetic fields turn out to be two aspects of a single field, mixed differently for different observers.

    The field of a moving charge

    Oliver Heaviside worked out in 1888 that the field of a charge moving steadily at speed \(v\) still points radially away from its present position, but is squeezed toward the plane perpendicular to the motion. With \(\beta = v/c\) and \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\),

    \[ E(r,\theta) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,\frac{1-\beta^2}{\left(1-\beta^2\sin^2\theta\right)^{3/2}}, \]

    so the field beside the charge is \(\gamma^3\) times stronger than the field ahead of it.

    The kink is the radiation

    Now stop the charge suddenly. News of the stop spreads outward at the speed of light. Inside a sphere of radius \(ct\) around the stopping point, the field is that of a charge at rest. Outside it, the field has not yet heard, and still points to where the charge would be had it kept going. Field lines cannot break, so in the thin shell between, each line runs sideways, almost perpendicular to the radius. J. J. Thomson drew this picture in 1904, and Edward Purcell made it the standard explanation in his 1965 textbook. The sideways field in the shell is the radiation. Its strength falls as \(1/r\), not \(1/r^2\), because the shell stretches sideways as it grows, so it carries energy to arbitrary distances. Joseph Larmor had derived the power radiated by an accelerating charge in 1897, \(P = q^2a^2/(6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3)\). The same physics makes X-rays when electrons stop in a tungsten target, as in the linear accelerators of Volume 6.

    Chapter 8 · Today

    Still the working theory

    Quantum electrodynamics, completed in the late 1940s, explains how light and matter exchange energy in quanta and predicts the electron's magnetic moment to about one part in a trillion. But for anything larger than an atom, classical electrodynamics remains the theory engineers use.

    • Computational electromagnetics. Yee's FDTD method, finite elements, and the method of moments solve Maxwell's equations for antennas, microwave circuits, chip interconnects, MRI coils, and the absorption of phone signals in the head. The hero and Fig. 4.1 use FDTD.
    • Metamaterials. Structures engineered smaller than a wavelength can give effective permittivity and permeability not found in nature. John Pendry predicted in 2000 that a material with negative refractive index could focus beyond the usual diffraction limit, and David Smith's group demonstrated negative refraction of microwaves in 2001. Cloaks that bend waves around an object have been demonstrated at microwave frequencies.
    • Photonics. Optical fibers, guided by total internal reflection, carry most of the world's data. Silicon photonics puts waveguides, modulators, and detectors on chips beside transistors.
    • Units. Since 1983 the meter has been defined by fixing the speed of light at 299,792,458 m/s. In the 2019 revision of the SI, the elementary charge was fixed exactly and \(\mu_0\), which had been exactly \(4\pi\times10^{-7}\) N/A², became a measured quantity; it agrees with the old value to about a part in ten billion.

    The common thread

    Faraday's lines of force began as a picture for someone who could not follow the mathematics. Maxwell took the picture seriously and found that the lines carry energy and momentum and can travel on their own as light. Every figure in this volume draws or computes those same lines: around charges, through coils, across a glass surface, and, in the end, as the kink that carries a charge's news to the far side of the universe.

    Glossary

    Displacement current
    Maxwell's term ε₀ ∂E/∂t, which acts as a source of magnetic field wherever the electric field changes.
    Electromotive force
    The voltage driving current around a circuit; in induction, minus the rate of change of magnetic flux.
    Equipotential
    A surface (a curve, in a plane) on which the electric potential is constant; field lines cross it at right angles.
    FDTD
    Finite-difference time-domain: solving Maxwell's equations on a staggered grid of electric and magnetic field points.
    Gaussian surface
    An imaginary closed surface used with Gauss's law to relate flux to enclosed charge.
    Grating lobe
    An unwanted extra beam of an antenna array whose elements are spaced more than half a wavelength apart.
    Helmholtz coil
    A pair of coaxial coils separated by their radius, giving a nearly uniform field between them.
    Lorentz force
    The force q(E + v × B) on a charge moving in electric and magnetic fields.
    Permittivity, permeability
    ε and μ, the constants relating fields to charges and currents in a medium; in vacuum ε₀ and μ₀.
    Refractive index
    The ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in a material.
