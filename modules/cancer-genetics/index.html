    Volume 15 · The genetics of cancer

    Two Hits

    A tumour is a clone of cells carrying a few mutations that mattered and a great many that did not. This volume follows how that sentence was established: counting tumours in children's eyes, reading the slope of an incidence curve, pulling mutational processes apart by their fingerprints, and calculating why a drug that works for a year stops working.

    Prologue

    A chromosome that was too small

    In 1960 Peter Nowell, a pathologist, and David Hungerford, a graduate student, were looking at chromosomes from the blood of patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia at the University of Pennsylvania and the Institute for Cancer Research in Philadelphia. In two patients, and then in seven, they found the same thing: one chromosome of the smallest pair was shorter than it should be. They reported it in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute that year, and the abnormality took the name of the city.

    This was the first consistent chromosomal change tied to a specific human cancer. It was also, for a while, a puzzle rather than an explanation. Nobody knew whether the small chromosome caused the leukaemia or was a consequence of it, and chromosome staining in 1960 was too crude to say what had been lost. In 1973 Janet Rowley, using the new quinacrine and Giemsa banding methods in Chicago, showed that nothing had been lost. The missing piece of chromosome 22 was stuck onto chromosome 9, and a piece of 9 onto 22: a reciprocal translocation, written \(t(9;22)\). By the early 1980s the breakpoint was mapped and the thing it created had a name, the fusion gene BCR-ABL1, and in 2001 a drug designed against the protein that fusion makes was approved.

    Forty-one years from a small chromosome to a pill. That arc is the subject of this volume, and it is worth stating at the start what the arc did and did not settle. Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells: a tumour is the descendants of one cell that acquired changes in its DNA, and those changes are what make its descendants grow when they should not. But a typical tumour genome carries thousands of mutations and only a handful of them are doing anything. Most of the intellectual work of cancer genetics, and most of this volume, is about telling those apart.

    Somatic mutation
    A change in the DNA of a body cell, present in that cell's descendants and in no one else. Distinguished from a germline variant, which is inherited and present in every cell.
    Driver
    A mutation that gives the cell carrying it a growth advantage, and so is enriched by natural selection within the tissue.
    Passenger
    A mutation that does nothing, carried along because it happened to be in a cell that was already on its way. The great majority.
    Clone
    A set of cells descended from one ancestor, sharing the mutations that ancestor had.
    Part I

    Counting the hits

    1971 Chapter 1 · Knudson's argument

    Two hits, inferred from children's eyes

    Retinoblastoma is a tumour of the developing retina, diagnosed in roughly one child in 16,000 to 18,000. It comes in two forms. Some children have tumours in both eyes, often several in each, diagnosed at a median age of about 15 months, and they pass the disease to their children as a dominant trait. Others have a single tumour in one eye, diagnosed later, at a median of about two years, with no family history. Alfred Knudson, at the M. D. Anderson Hospital in Houston, had 48 cases of retinoblastoma and the published literature, and he asked what one model could produce both patterns.

    His answer, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 1971, was that two mutational events are needed in one retinal cell. In the hereditary form the first is already present in every cell of the body, inherited from a parent, so only one more event is required and it can happen independently in many cells. In the non-hereditary form both events must occur in the same cell, which is far less likely and happens at most once.

    Why the ages differ

    The argument is about kinetics. Suppose a cell suffers the required event at a constant rate. If only one event is needed, the fraction of eventual cases that have not yet appeared by age \(t\) falls exponentially:

    \[ S_1(t) = e^{-t/\tau}, \]

    a straight line on a logarithmic plot. If two events are needed in the same cell, the probability of being affected by \(t\) grows as \(t^2\) rather than \(t\), and the corresponding curve bends. Knudson plotted the fraction of cases not yet diagnosed against age for bilateral and unilateral retinoblastoma and found exactly that: the bilateral cases fell on a straight line, the unilateral cases on a curve. One hit for the carriers, two for everyone else.

    Why three tumours

    The second half of the argument is about counting. If a carrier's retinal cells each acquire the second event independently and rarely, the number of tumours in a carrier is Poisson distributed with some mean \(m\):

    \[ P(k \text{ tumours}) = \frac{m^k e^{-m}}{k!}. \]

    Knudson found \(m \approx 3\) fitted the observed tumour counts. That one number then predicts the rest of the clinical picture without further adjustment. Split the \(m\) tumours between two eyes and an eye is spared with probability \(e^{-m/2}\), so a carrier has no tumour at all with probability \(e^{-m} = 5\%\), disease in one eye only with probability \(2e^{-m/2}(1 - e^{-m/2}) = 35\%\), and disease in both eyes with probability \((1 - e^{-m/2})^2 = 60\%\). Carriers who escape entirely are rare but real, which is why retinoblastoma pedigrees occasionally skip a generation, and the measured penetrance of an RB1 mutation is above 90% rather than 100%.

    Knudson's paper contains no molecular biology. It is an argument from the shapes of two curves and a count of tumours, and it was right. Fifteen years later the gene was cloned and the first hit turned out to be a deletion or inactivating mutation in one copy of RB1 on chromosome 13, the second the loss of the remaining copy. The model also made a prediction about what sort of gene this had to be. If one working copy is enough for a normal cell and cancer follows only when both are gone, the gene is not an accelerator that gets jammed on. It is a brake, and both brakes have to fail. In pedigrees the disease looks dominant, because carriers have one brake already gone in every cell; at the level of a cell it is recessive.

    1982 Chapter 2 · Oncogenes

    The other kind of gene: a switch stuck on

    The brake genes were inferred before they were found. The accelerators were found first, and in a virus. In 1911 Peyton Rous showed that a filtered extract from a chicken sarcoma gave sarcomas to other chickens. The agent became Rous sarcoma virus, and in 1970 Peter Duesberg and Peter Vogt showed it carried a gene the virus did not need for its own replication, src. In 1976 Dominique Stehelin, Harold Varmus, J. Michael Bishop and Peter Vogt found a close relative of src in normal chicken DNA. The virus had not invented a cancer gene; it had stolen a normal one.

    That reframed the question. If cancer genes are corrupted versions of genes everyone has, a human tumour should contain one. In 1982 three laboratories, Robert Weinberg's at MIT, Mariano Barbacid's at the National Cancer Institute and Michael Wigler's at Cold Spring Harbor, took DNA from human tumour cell lines, put it into mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblasts, and asked which fragment made the recipient cells grow in a disordered heap. The fragment was the human homologue of the Harvey rat sarcoma gene, HRAS. The difference between the tumour copy and the normal copy was one base: glycine to valine at codon 12.

    What a single codon does

    RAS proteins are molecular switches. They are on when bound to GTP and off when bound to GDP, and they hydrolyse their own GTP to turn themselves off. The hydrolysis is slow, about \(3 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so on its own RAS would stay on for an hour. A family of GTPase-activating proteins, GAPs, accelerate it by more than a hundred thousand fold, to about \(19\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Exchange factors push the cycle the other way, pulling GDP out so GTP can bind. In a resting cell the GAPs win and almost all RAS is off.

    If exchange runs at rate \(k_{\mathrm{ex}}\) and the total hydrolysis rate is \(k_{\mathrm{hyd}}\), the fraction of RAS in the active state at steady state is

    \[ f_{\mathrm{GTP}} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{ex}}}{k_{\mathrm{ex}} + k_{\mathrm{hyd}}}, \qquad k_{\mathrm{hyd}} = k_{\mathrm{int}} + [\mathrm{GAP}]\,k_{\mathrm{GAP}}. \]

    A mutation at codon 12 or 61 sits in the active site. It barely affects nucleotide binding, and it slows intrinsic hydrolysis somewhat, but what it mainly does is make the protein a poor substrate for GAP. Jonathan Hunter and colleagues measured this in 2015 for the common KRAS mutants. Relative to wild type, intrinsic hydrolysis runs at 28% for G12D, 6% for G12V and 1% for Q61L, while GAP-stimulated hydrolysis runs at 2.1%, 0.6% and 0.3%. The switch is not broken open. It is deaf to the thing that normally shuts it.

    So cancer genetics has two kinds of gene and two kinds of mutation. An oncogene is activated: the change is a gain of function, it is dominant, one copy is enough, and the mutations cluster at a few specific codons because only a few changes jam the switch. A tumour suppressor is inactivated: the change is a loss of function, it is recessive in the cell, both copies must go, and the mutations are scattered all over the gene because almost any truncation will do. That asymmetry is visible in sequencing data and is one of the strongest signals for deciding what an unfamiliar gene is doing. KRAS mutations in colorectal cancer are overwhelmingly at codons 12, 13 and 61. APC mutations are nonsense and frameshift changes spread across 8,538 bases of coding sequence.

    1986 Chapter 3 · Tumour suppressors

    How the second copy goes

    Knudson's second hit is usually not a second point mutation. It is the loss of the chromosome segment carrying the remaining good copy, and that loss leaves a signature in the data that was exploited long before anyone could sequence a gene.

    The method is called loss of heterozygosity. Pick a marker near the suspected gene where the patient is heterozygous, one allele from each parent, and compare normal tissue with tumour. If the tumour has lost one parental copy of that region, the marker reads as homozygous in the tumour and heterozygous in the blood. Webster Cavenee, Ray White and colleagues did this for retinoblastoma in 1983 and showed that the tumours had lost one copy of chromosome 13q, and that in hereditary cases the copy lost was the one inherited from the unaffected parent, leaving the mutant copy behind. In 1986 Stephen Friend, Thaddeus Dryja, Weinberg and colleagues cloned the gene itself; in 1987 Dryja's group showed it was mutated in the germline of hereditary cases. In 1989 the same logic applied to chromosome 17p led Bert Vogelstein's group to TP53, a gene that had been known since 1979 but misfiled as an oncogene, because the mutant form had been studied first.

    Reading it out of read counts

    Modern sequencing measures the same thing by counting reads. At a heterozygous site, the fraction of reads carrying the B allele, the \(B\)-allele frequency, is a mixture of what the tumour cells contribute and what the normal cells in the sample contribute. With tumour purity \(\rho\), tumour copy number \(n_A + n_B\) at that locus, and diploid normal cells, the expected value is

    \[ \mathrm{BAF} = \frac{\rho\, n_B + (1 - \rho)}{\rho\,(n_A + n_B) + 2(1 - \rho)} . \]

    Set \(n_A = n_B = 1\) and this is \(1/2\) whatever the purity: a balanced locus looks balanced. Set \(n_A = 1, n_B = 0\), a deletion of one copy, and it becomes \((1-\rho)/(2-\rho)\), which falls from \(1/2\) at zero purity to \(0\) at purity one. The depth of coverage tells you the other half of the story: a locus with one copy instead of two in the tumour carries a read depth ratio of \((\rho \cdot 1 + 2(1-\rho))/2\), while a copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, where one parental copy is lost and the other duplicated, has the allelic imbalance with no change in depth at all.

    Loss of heterozygosity is also why cancer genomes are so often aneuploid. Losing one copy of a chromosome arm is a cheap way to delete a brake, and the same event takes out every other gene on that arm as collateral. Something like a quarter of a typical solid tumour genome has lost a parental copy somewhere. Whole-genome doubling, which happens in about a third of tumours, often follows a loss, and the doubled karyotype preserves the imbalance.

    1990 Chapter 4 · The multistage model

    How many steps, counted from an incidence curve

    Two hits explain retinoblastoma, a tumour of a tissue that stops dividing in infancy. Adult carcinomas need more, and the number can be read off a public health statistic that was collected for other reasons entirely.

    In 1954 Peter Armitage and Richard Doll, at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, noticed that the age-specific incidence of most carcinomas rises as a high power of age. If a cell must pass through \(k\) ordered rare steps, each at constant rate, the probability that it has finished by age \(t\) grows as \(t^k\), and the incidence rate, its derivative, grows as

    \[ I(t) \;\propto\; t^{\,k-1}. \]

    On a plot of \(\log I\) against \(\log t\) that is a straight line of slope \(k - 1\). Armitage and Doll found slopes between five and six for several common cancers and concluded that six or seven steps were needed. Their model was too simple in detail, because it ignores that the intermediate cells divide, and a two-stage model with clonal expansion of the intermediate population, worked out by Suresh Moolgavkar and Alfred Knudson in 1981, can produce the same curve with fewer stages. The exponent still sets a floor on how much has to happen.

    The steps, named

    In 1990 Eric Fearon and Bert Vogelstein put names to the steps for one cancer. Comparing normal colon, small adenomas, large adenomas and carcinomas from the same patients, they found an ordered accumulation: loss of APC at the earliest stage, often before any visible lesion; activation of KRAS in the growing adenoma; loss of chromosome 18q, later shown to involve SMAD4; and loss of TP53 at the transition to carcinoma. The order was statistical rather than obligatory, and they said so, but the picture of a tumour as a sequence of selected clonal expansions has held up. Each step is a mutation that lets a clone outgrow its neighbours, which is Peter Nowell's 1976 proposal that a tumour evolves by clonal selection, made concrete.

    The number of steps and the number of mutations are not the same thing. Iñigo Martincorena and colleagues counted selection directly in 2017, by borrowing the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions, \(dN/dS\), from molecular evolution. In species evolution the ratio is below one almost everywhere, because most protein changes are harmful. In cancer it is above one in a few hundred genes, because the mutations that change proteins in those genes are the ones being selected. Across 7,664 tumours of 29 types they found an average of about four coding substitutions per tumour under positive selection, from fewer than one in thyroid and testicular cancer to more than ten in endometrial and colorectal cancer. Four drivers, in a genome carrying thousands of mutations.

    Part II

    Reading the genome

    1993 Chapter 5 · Mutator phenotypes

    Tumours that break the copier

    In 1993 three groups, working on families with inherited colorectal cancer, found something that did not fit the pattern of brakes and accelerators. The tumours carried extra and missing copies of short repeated sequences, scattered across the genome, at thousands of sites. Manuel Perucho's group, Bert Vogelstein's group with Stanislas Thibodeau's, and Lauri Aaltonen with Albert de la Chapelle all reported it within months. The tumours were not just mutated. They were mutating, continuously, because a repair system had failed.

    The system is mismatch repair. DNA polymerase slips when copying a run of identical bases, adding or dropping one unit of the repeat, and mismatch repair recognises the resulting loop and fixes it. When the genes that do this are lost, usually MLH1 or MSH2, slippage goes uncorrected and every microsatellite in the genome drifts. The resulting state is called microsatellite instability, MSI, and the inherited syndrome that causes it is Lynch syndrome.

    Drift in a repeat tract

    Model a tract of \(L\) repeat units in one cell lineage. At each division it gains or loses one unit with probability \(p\) per division, and repair catches a fraction \(1 - \epsilon\) of those events, so the effective step rate is \(p\epsilon\) per division. The tract length performs a random walk, and after \(n\) divisions its length has standard deviation

    \[ \sigma_L = \sqrt{n\,p\,\epsilon}. \]

    The slippage rate itself rises steeply with tract length, roughly exponentially, which is why the markers chosen for clinical testing are long mononucleotide runs. The Bethesda panel settled on BAT25 and BAT26, runs of 25 and 26 adenines, plus three dinucleotide markers. A tumour is called MSI-high when two or more of five markers have shifted.

    A mutator phenotype is not confined to mismatch repair. Proofreading-deficient DNA polymerase epsilon, from an exonuclease-domain mutation in POLE, produces tumours with more than a hundred mutations per megabase, the highest burdens seen outside melanoma. Loss of BRCA1 or BRCA2 breaks homologous recombination and leaves a characteristic pattern of deletions at microhomologies. These states matter clinically for a reason that would have surprised anyone in 1993: a genome full of mutations makes a lot of abnormal protein fragments, and those are visible to T cells. In May 2017 the US Food and Drug Administration approved pembrolizumab, an antibody against PD-1, for any advanced solid tumour that is mismatch-repair deficient, regardless of where in the body it started. It was the first approval in oncology written for a genotype rather than an organ.

    2013 Chapter 6 · Mutational signatures

    Every process leaves a different fingerprint

    A mutation is not just a substitution. It is a substitution in a context, and different damaging processes prefer different contexts. Ultraviolet light makes dimers between adjacent pyrimidines, so its mutations are C to T where the neighbouring base is also a pyrimidine. Benzo[a]pyrene from tobacco smoke sticks to guanine, which is read as C to A. Spontaneous loss of an amino group from methylated cytosine happens at CpG sites, giving C to T at ACG, CCG, GCG and TCG.

    In 2013 Ludmil Alexandrov, Serena Nik-Zainal, Michael Stratton and colleagues turned this into a method. Classify every substitution in a tumour by its change and the bases on either side. There are six substitution classes once you fold each change together with its complement, and sixteen trinucleotide contexts for each, so 96 categories. A tumour's catalogue is a vector of 96 counts, and a catalogue produced by several processes at once is a non-negative mixture of their individual profiles. Factorise a matrix of many tumours' catalogues into a small number of non-negative components and the components are the processes.

    \[ M \;\approx\; S\,E, \qquad M \in \mathbb{R}^{96 \times t}_{\ge 0},\; S \in \mathbb{R}^{96 \times s}_{\ge 0},\; E \in \mathbb{R}^{s \times t}_{\ge 0}. \]

    The extracted components turned out to be interpretable. Signature 7 appears only in sun-exposed skin. Signature 4 appears in smokers' lungs and not in non-smokers'. Signature 1 appears in everything and in proportion to age. The 2020 pan-cancer analysis of 4,645 whole genomes and 19,184 exomes, 84.7 million mutations in all, extended the catalogue to 49 single-base signatures, 11 doublet, 4 clustered and 17 indel signatures. The catalogue, COSMIC, is now a reference set, and the usual task is no longer extraction but refitting: given one tumour's 96 counts, which known signatures explain them, and in what proportion.

    The method has had two kinds of use. The first is forensic: a liver tumour whose spectrum is almost pure T to A at CTG has been exposed to aristolochic acid, a compound in some herbal remedies, and that finding turned up in Taiwanese liver cancers where nobody had been looking. The second is prognostic in a narrow sense. The flat signature of homologous-recombination failure marks tumours that cannot repair double-strand breaks, and those tumours are the ones that respond to PARP inhibitors. Mutational signatures turned a drug-selection problem into a pattern-matching problem.

    2013 Chapter 7 · Drivers and passengers

    The gene that is mutated because it is long

    Sequence a few hundred tumours, count how many have a mutation in each gene, and the genes at the top of the list will include TP53, KRAS and PIK3CA. They will also include TTN, which encodes titin, a muscle protein with 101,862 bases of coding sequence, and several olfactory receptors. Titin is not a cancer gene. It is a very large target.

    The test that is supposed to catch this is simple enough. If a gene of length \(\ell\) accumulates mutations at the background rate \(\mu\) per base per tumour, the number of mutated tumours out of \(T\) is binomial, and the \(p\)-value for observing \(x\) or more is

    \[ P(X \ge x) = \sum_{i=x}^{T} \binom{T}{i}\,q^{\,i}(1-q)^{\,T-i}, \qquad q = 1 - e^{-\mu \ell}. \]

    Correct for testing twenty thousand genes at once with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure and report the genes whose \(q\)-value falls below a threshold. Run this with one \(\mu\) for the whole genome and titin is significant, because the real background rate is not uniform. Michael Lawrence and colleagues showed in 2013 why. The mutation rate varies more than fivefold across the genome and is strongly predicted by two things: how late in S phase a region replicates, and how highly it is transcribed. Late-replicating, silent regions mutate far more. Titin in a carcinoma is late-replicating and barely expressed; TP53 is early and highly expressed. Their method, MutSigCV, estimates a gene-specific background rate from those covariates and from each patient's own mutation rate and spectrum, and the false positives disappear.

    Counting mutated tumours per gene is only one of several tests, and the others use different evidence. Clustering of mutations at a single codon is strong evidence for an activating oncogene even when the total count is modest. An excess of truncating mutations spread evenly through a gene is evidence for a tumour suppressor. The \(dN/dS\) approach tests for selection without needing a background model at all, by using synonymous mutations in the same gene as the control. Current driver catalogues are built from the agreement among several such methods, and the PCAWG consortium ran six of them side by side on 2,658 whole genomes in 2020 for precisely that reason.

    2012 Chapter 8 · Clonal architecture

    A tumour is not one genome

    Grind up a tumour, sequence it, and the answer is an average over however many distinct clones were in the piece you took. In 2012 Marco Gerlinger and Charles Swanton sequenced several regions from each of four kidney tumours and found that about two thirds of the mutations were not shared between all regions of the same tumour. Most of what the biopsy reports is specific to the part of the tumour the needle happened to reach.

    The numbers that reveal this are in the read counts. A mutation present in every tumour cell on one of two chromosome copies should appear in half the tumour-derived reads. One present in a quarter of the tumour cells should appear in an eighth. Converting back requires undoing both the normal-cell dilution and the local copy number, which gives the cancer cell fraction:

    \[ \mathrm{CCF} = \mathrm{VAF} \cdot \frac{\rho\,(n_A + n_B) + 2\,(1 - \rho)}{\rho\,m}, \]

    where \(m\) is the number of tumour copies that carry the mutation, usually one. A histogram of CCF across the mutations in a tumour then has a peak near 1, the clonal mutations present in the founding cell, and one or more peaks below it, each a subclone. Fit a mixture to that histogram and you have the clonal structure; order the subclones by which mutations they share and you have a tree.

    Clonal structure is not an academic refinement. The TRACERx study followed hundreds of lung cancers from surgery onward and found that the degree of chromosomal instability between regions of the primary tumour predicted relapse. And a subclone is what a drug meets: the mutation that will cause resistance is often already present in a few cells at diagnosis, below the detection limit of any single biopsy. That is the subject of the next chapter.

    Part III

    Acting on it

    2001 Chapter 9 · Targeted therapy

    Why a drug that works stops working

    Imatinib was designed against the kinase that the Philadelphia translocation creates. In the IRIS trial, newly diagnosed chronic myeloid leukaemia patients given imatinib had an overall survival of 83% at ten years, in a disease whose median survival had been about five years. It is the best case in targeted oncology, and the reason it is the best case is not that the drug is uniquely good. It is that chronic myeloid leukaemia at diagnosis is driven by one lesion in a small number of cells.

    In advanced solid tumours the pattern is different. Responses to a well-matched targeted drug are often dramatic and almost always temporary. Relapse comes with a mutation that blocks the drug: BCR-ABL1 T315I under imatinib, EGFR T790M under gefitinib, KRAS mutations under the anti-EGFR antibodies in colorectal cancer. In 2012 Luis Diaz and colleagues sequenced plasma from patients receiving panitumumab and found the resistant KRAS clones appearing months before the scans changed, and in some patients detectable before treatment started. Mathematical modelling of the same cohort said the clones were there in essentially everyone: they were not caused by the drug, they were revealed by it.

    The arithmetic of pre-existing resistance

    Ivana Bozic, Martin Nowak and colleagues worked out the expected number of resistant cells present when treatment begins. A tumour of \(M\) cells grows with birth rate \(b\) and death rate \(d\), so the net growth fraction is \(s = (b-d)/b\). If \(n\) distinct point mutations can each confer resistance, and the point mutation rate is \(u\) per base per division, the expected number of resistant cells at size \(M\) is

    \[ X \;\approx\; M\,n\,\mu, \qquad \mu = u\,s\,\log(Ms), \]

    and the probability that none exists is \(e^{-X}\). The logarithm is the price of history: resistant lineages that arose early have had the longest time to grow, so the expected number is larger than a naive \(Mnu\). For a second drug with \(n_2\) independent resistance mutations, a cell must carry two, and the expected number falls to \(M n_1 n_2 \mu^2\). Unless one single mutation confers resistance to both, in which case the count is back to \(M n_{12} \mu\) and the second drug buys nothing.

    Two other strategies attack the problem from different angles. Synthetic lethality exploits a repair pathway the tumour has already lost: a cell without BRCA1 or BRCA2 depends on PARP-mediated repair to survive, so a PARP inhibitor kills it and spares normal cells, as Thomas Helleday's and Alan Ashworth's groups showed in 2005. Immune checkpoint blockade attacks the passengers rather than the drivers: a hypermutated tumour presents many abnormal peptides, and releasing the brakes on T cells lets them act on what is already visible. Neither escapes evolution. BRCA-mutant tumours relapse on PARP inhibitors by restoring the reading frame of the broken gene, and hypermutated tumours relapse by losing antigen presentation.

    Now Chapter 10 · Finding it early

    Looking for a tumour in a tube of blood

    Cells that die shed their DNA into the bloodstream in fragments about the length of a nucleosome's wrap. A tumour does this too, and the tumour-derived fraction of plasma cell-free DNA carries the tumour's mutations. Detecting it is a sampling problem before it is a sequencing problem, and the sampling limit is hard.

    A millilitre of plasma typically yields five to thirty nanograms of cell-free DNA. One haploid human genome weighs 3.3 picograms, so that is roughly 1,500 to 9,000 genome copies: a few thousand molecules at each position in the genome, and no more. If the tumour fraction is \(f\) and a mutation is heterozygous in the tumour, the chance that any one molecule covering that position carries it is \(f/2\). With \(N\) molecules per site and \(S\) sites being watched, the number of mutant molecules recovered is binomial with mean \(NSf/2\), and the probability of seeing at least \(k\) of them is

    \[ P(\ge k) = \sum_{i=k}^{NS} \binom{NS}{i} \left(\frac{f}{2}\right)^{i}\left(1 - \frac{f}{2}\right)^{NS - i}. \]

    At a tumour fraction of \(10^{-4}\) with 3,000 genome copies, a single tracked mutation gives an expected yield of 0.15 molecules. No amount of sequencing depth helps, because the molecules are not in the tube. The only remedies are more plasma, or more sites: watch 500 mutations instead of one and the expected yield is 75. That is why minimal-residual-disease assays are tumour-informed, built from a panel of hundreds or thousands of mutations found by sequencing the patient's own resected tumour first.

    These assays are now used in three ways, with very different levels of evidence behind them. Choosing a drug for advanced disease from plasma instead of a biopsy is established practice; the question is only whether the assay found the mutation, and the tumour fraction in advanced disease is often above a per cent. Detecting residual disease after surgery is the most promising use and the subject of many running trials: a positive result weeks after resection identifies patients who will relapse, long before imaging can. Screening people without symptoms is the hardest of the three, because the tumour fraction is lowest exactly when detection would help most, and because a test applied to millions of healthy people must have an extraordinarily low false-positive rate to do more good than harm. The arithmetic in the figure above is the reason the first two work and the third is still being argued about.

    What the genome does not say

    It is worth ending on the limits. A list of driver mutations does not predict how a tumour will behave, and two tumours with the same drivers often behave differently. Much of what distinguishes them is epigenetic, or lies in which cell type the tumour started from, or in the stroma and immune cells around it. Mutations in non-coding regions, which make up 98% of the genome, remain mostly uninterpretable: the PCAWG analysis of 2,658 whole genomes found very few non-coding drivers besides the TERT promoter. And the mutations are not confined to tumours. Normal oesophagus in a middle-aged person carries clones with NOTCH1 mutations covering much of its surface; normal skin carries clones with the same drivers as the cancers that arise in it. Having the mutations is common. Becoming a cancer is not.

    The shape of the field now

    Cancer genetics began by counting tumours in children's eyes and ended up with the most thoroughly catalogued set of mutational processes in biology. What it has not delivered is a general cure, and the reason is in Chapter 9 rather than in any gap in the catalogue. A tumour is a population under selection, and a population under selection escapes a single pressure. The treatments that work best are the ones that attack something the tumour cannot change: a lesion it depends on absolutely, a repair pathway it has already lost, or an immune system that adapts as fast as the tumour does.

    Reference

    Glossary

    Aneuploidy
    An abnormal number of chromosomes or chromosome arms in a cell.
    B-allele frequency
    The fraction of reads at a heterozygous site carrying the non-reference allele; its departure from one half reveals allelic imbalance.
    Cancer cell fraction
    The fraction of the tumour's cells that carry a given mutation, obtained from the variant allele frequency after correcting for purity and copy number.
    Cell-free DNA
    Fragments of DNA circulating in plasma, shed by dying cells. The tumour-derived part is called circulating tumour DNA.
    Clonal haematopoiesis
    Expanded blood-cell clones carrying driver mutations in a person without leukaemia; a common source of false positives in plasma tests.
    Copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity
    Loss of one parental copy of a region with duplication of the other, so the total copy number is unchanged but one parent's alleles are gone.
    Driver
    A mutation that confers a selective growth advantage on the cell carrying it.
    dN/dS
    The ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitution rates; above one indicates positive selection.
    Loss of heterozygosity
    Loss of one parental allele at a locus where the person is heterozygous, typically by deletion or by mitotic recombination.
    Microsatellite instability
    Widespread length changes in short repeat tracts, caused by failure of mismatch repair.
    Mutational signature
    The characteristic distribution of substitution types and sequence contexts produced by one mutational process.
    Oncogene
    A gene whose activation promotes cancer; the mutations are gain-of-function, dominant, and clustered at particular codons.
    Penetrance
    The fraction of carriers of a germline variant who develop the associated disease.
    Purity
    The fraction of cells in a sequenced sample that are tumour cells.
    Synthetic lethality
    A combination of two defects that kills a cell although either alone is tolerated; the basis of PARP inhibitors in BRCA-mutant cancer.
    Tumour mutational burden
    The number of somatic mutations per megabase of sequenced DNA.
    Tumour suppressor
    A gene whose loss promotes cancer; the mutations are loss-of-function, recessive in the cell, and scattered through the gene.
    Variant allele frequency
    The fraction of sequencing reads at a site that carry the mutant allele.
