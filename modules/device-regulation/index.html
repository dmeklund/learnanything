    Cleared for Market

    How the United States and the European Union decide which medical devices and diagnostic tests may be sold: what counts as a device, how risk sets the class, the routes to market, the quality and risk systems behind every product, the statistics of diagnostic accuracy, the special problems of software, and the surveillance that continues after launch.

    Prologue

    From tongue depressors to pacemakers

    A medical device is anything used to diagnose, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the body's structure or function, that does its main job physically rather than by chemistry or metabolism. That last clause is what separates devices from drugs. The definition covers bandages and MRI scanners, contact lenses and artificial hearts, smartphone apps and the reagents in a COVID-19 test. Diagnostics that test specimens taken from the body, such as blood, urine, or tissue, are in vitro diagnostic devices (IVDs), and both jurisdictions in this volume treat them with rules of their own.

    Because devices range from trivial to life-sustaining, every modern system regulates them by risk. A tongue depressor needs little more than honest labeling and a clean factory. A pacemaker needs clinical evidence, an inspection of the plant that makes it, and lifelong tracking of every unit implanted. The figure above shows the same five products under the US and EU systems, which reach similar answers by different routes. This volume explains how those answers are reached.

    This volume explains the structure of the rules as of 2026. It is not legal or regulatory advice; actual classification depends on a product's specific intended use and on current guidance.

    Part I

    The United States

    1976 Chapter 1 · The Medical Device Amendments

    The Dalkon Shield and the three classes

    The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, passed after the Elixir Sulfanilamide poisonings, brought devices under federal law for the first time, but only against adulteration and misbranding. The FDA could act against a dangerous device once it was on the market; it could not review one beforehand.

    By the late 1960s, implants, pacemakers, and dialysis machines had made that gap serious. In 1970 a committee chaired by Theodore Cooper, director of the National Heart and Lung Institute, recommended that devices be regulated according to their risk. The case that forced action was the Dalkon Shield, an intrauterine contraceptive device sold from 1971 and implanted in some two million American women, which was linked to pelvic infections, infertility, and deaths. On 28 May 1976 President Gerald Ford signed the Medical Device Amendments.

    Risk sets the class

    The amendments sort devices into three classes, by the controls needed to assure safety and effectiveness:

    • Class I, general controls: registration, listing, labeling, good manufacturing practice, and adverse event reporting. Most Class I devices, such as bandages and tongue depressors, need no premarket review.
    • Class II, general plus special controls: performance standards, specific testing, labeling, or postmarket studies written for that device type. Most need a 510(k).
    • Class III, devices that sustain life, are implanted with significant risk, or present unreasonable risk, for which general and special controls cannot provide assurance. These need premarket approval.

    FDA panels then wrote some 1,700 classification regulations, in 21 CFR Parts 862 to 892, each naming a generic device type and its class. A new product finds its place by matching one of them. Devices already on the market in 1976 were "grandfathered," and new devices "substantially equivalent" to them could reach the market by a notice under section 510(k) of the Act. That provision, intended as a transition, became the main road to market.

    1997 Chapter 2 · Premarket review

    510(k), De Novo, and PMA

    The FDA receives about three thousand 510(k) notifications a year, against a few dozen original premarket approval applications. The routes differ in the question asked:

    • 510(k) premarket notification. Is the device substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate: the same intended use, and either the same technological characteristics or different ones that raise no new questions of safety and effectiveness? Evidence is usually bench testing, sometimes clinical data. The FDA "clears" the device.
    • De Novo. Introduced by the FDA Modernization Act of 1997 and streamlined in 2012, it lets a novel low- or moderate-risk device with no predicate be classified directly into Class I or II, with new special controls. The granted device then becomes a predicate for followers.
    • Premarket approval (PMA). Is there valid scientific evidence, usually clinical trials, of reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness? The FDA inspects manufacturing, often convenes an advisory panel, and "approves" the device. Later changes need PMA supplements.
    • Humanitarian Device Exemption. For conditions affecting no more than 8,000 people a year in the US (raised from 4,000 by the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016), it requires probable benefit instead of proof of effectiveness.

    The 510(k) route has drawn persistent criticism, because equivalence to a predicate is not proof of safety. A 2011 Institute of Medicine report concluded that it could not be transformed into a premarket evaluation of safety and effectiveness. The DePuy ASR metal-on-metal hip, cleared by 510(k) and recalled in 2010 after high failure rates, became the standard example. Defenders point out that most 510(k) devices are low-risk and well understood.

    User fees and the review clock

    Since the Medical Device User Fee and Modernization Act of 2002, manufacturers pay fees that fund review staff, and every five years the FDA and industry negotiate performance goals. Under the current agreement, MDUFA V (fiscal years 2023 to 2027), the FDA aims to decide 95% of 510(k)s within 90 "FDA days," 70% of De Novo requests within 150, and 90% of PMAs without an advisory panel within 180. FDA days exclude the time a submission sits on hold while the sponsor answers an additional-information request, so calendar time is always longer. A sponsor who does not respond within 180 days is considered to have withdrawn the submission.

    Since 2018 the Breakthrough Devices Program has offered more interaction and priority review to devices that could provide more effective treatment or diagnosis of life-threatening or irreversibly debilitating conditions.

    1996 Chapter 3 · Quality systems and risk management

    Design controls and the risk file

    Premarket review looks at a design. A quality system makes sure every unit built matches it. The FDA's first device good manufacturing practice regulation took effect in 1978. In 1996 it was replaced by the Quality System Regulation, 21 CFR Part 820, which added design controls: documented design inputs, outputs, reviews, verification that the design meets its specifications, and validation that the finished device meets the user's needs. Investigations of device failures had found that many traced to design, not manufacturing.

    Internationally, the equivalent standard is ISO 13485, first published in 1996 and revised in 2003 and 2016. On 2 February 2026 the FDA's Quality Management System Regulation took effect, incorporating ISO 13485:2016 into Part 820 by reference, so a single quality system can now satisfy both the US and most other markets. Since 2017 the Medical Device Single Audit Program lets one audit serve the regulators of the US, Canada, Brazil, Japan, and Australia.

    ISO 14971: risk management

    ISO 14971 (first edition 2000, current edition 2019) sets out the process every device manufacturer follows. Identify hazards and the sequences of events by which they could cause harm. Estimate each risk from the severity of the harm and its probability. Compare with acceptability criteria the manufacturer must define in advance. Reduce unacceptable risks with controls, chosen in a fixed order of priority: first inherent safety by design, then protective measures in the device or its manufacture, and only then information for safety, such as warnings and training. Finally, judge whether the overall residual risk is outweighed by the medical benefit, and keep the risk file alive with data from the field.

    The order matters because warnings are the weakest control: people do not read labels, and use error causes a large share of device incidents. A dose limit enforced by hardware works whether or not anyone reads anything.

    1988 Chapter 4 · In vitro diagnostics

    Tests, labs, and the numbers behind a claim

    A diagnostic test kit is a device; the laboratory that runs it is regulated separately. In 1988, after reports of errors in Pap smear screening, Congress passed the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA), administered by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. CLIA certifies laboratories and sorts tests by complexity: waived tests simple enough for home or a doctor's office, and moderate- and high-complexity tests that need qualified staff, proficiency testing, and inspection.

    That split left a gap. A test designed, made, and used within a single laboratory, a laboratory-developed test (LDT), was for decades left by the FDA to CLIA alone under a policy of enforcement discretion. The scandal at Theranos, whose blood tests failed in its own laboratories before CMS sanctioned it in 2016, sharpened the debate. In May 2024 the FDA issued a rule declaring LDTs to be devices and phasing in its oversight. On 31 March 2025 a federal court in the Eastern District of Texas vacated the rule, holding that Congress had assigned laboratory oversight to CLIA; the FDA did not appeal and formally rescinded the rule in September 2025.

    Analytical and clinical performance

    A diagnostic must show analytical performance, measuring what it claims accurately and reproducibly, and clinical performance, agreeing with the true condition of patients. For a yes/no test, clinical performance is summarized by

    \[ \text{sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad \text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \]

    where TP, FN, TN, and FP count true positives, false negatives, true negatives, and false positives against a reference method. A regulator cares less about the point estimate than about how precisely it is known. A common acceptance criterion requires the lower bound of a 95% confidence interval to exceed a target. With \(x\) correct results out of \(n\), the Wilson score interval is

    \[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}, \qquad \hat p = \frac{x}{n},\ z = 1.96. \]

    Its width shrinks only as \(1/\sqrt n\), so tightening a claim gets expensive fast, and for rare conditions the number of positive samples, not the total enrolled, is what limits a study.

    Why a good test can mostly give wrong positives

    Sensitivity and specificity describe the test. What a patient or doctor wants to know is the chance that a particular result is right, the predictive value, and that depends on how common the condition is among those tested. By Bayes' theorem, with prevalence \(\pi\),

    \[ \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens}\cdot\pi}{\text{sens}\cdot\pi + (1-\text{spec})(1-\pi)}. \]

    This is why regulators look closely at a test's intended population. The same assay may be cleared for diagnosing people with symptoms but not for screening the general public, and why the EU's IVDR puts tests for self-testing and for screening blood donations in higher classes.

    2014 Chapter 5 · Software as a medical device

    When the device is an algorithm

    Software has controlled devices for decades, and its failures have shaped the rules: the Therac-25 overdoses of the 1980s (Volume 6) are a standard lesson in software safety. Increasingly, though, software is the whole device: an app that analyses heart rhythm, a program that reads retinal photographs, an algorithm that flags strokes on CT scans.

    In 2014 the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) published a framework for software as a medical device (SaMD) that rates risk on two axes: how serious the patient's situation is (critical, serious, non-serious) and how much the software's output matters to the decision (it treats or diagnoses, drives clinical management, or informs it). The EU's MDR wrote a dedicated classification rule, Rule 11, which puts most decision-support software in Class IIa or above; guidance document MDCG 2019-11 maps it onto the IMDRF grid. Software development follows IEC 62304, the subject of the next chapter.

    In the US, the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016 removed some software from the device definition, including administrative tools, general wellness apps, electronic health records, and certain clinical decision support that lets a professional independently review the basis for its recommendations. In February 2018 the FDA granted a De Novo to Viz.ai's ContaCT, which flags suspected large-vessel strokes on CT and alerts a specialist, creating a new device type for radiological computer-aided triage. The FDA now lists more than a thousand AI-enabled devices it has authorized, most of them in radiology. Because machine-learning models may be retrained, the FDA finalized guidance in December 2024 on predetermined change control plans, which let a manufacturer describe in its submission which model updates it will make and how it will validate them, so those changes need no new review.

    2006 Chapter 6 · IEC 62304

    A life cycle for medical device software

    Regulators cannot test software into safety. A program with a few hundred thousand lines has more paths than any test campaign can cover, and the failures that hurt patients, such as race conditions, unhandled inputs, and an untested update, often hide in paths nobody exercised. The accepted answer, from aviation and nuclear software before medicine, is to regulate the process: require a disciplined life cycle whose rigor scales with what a failure could do.

    For medical devices that process is IEC 62304, Medical device software: Software life cycle processes, published in 2006 by the International Electrotechnical Commission's subcommittee on medical electrical equipment together with ISO's committee on quality management for devices. Amendment 1 (2015) rewrote the safety classification, made system testing mandatory for every class, and added a route for legacy software already on the market. The FDA recognizes IEC 62304 as a consensus standard, and its European version, EN 62304, is the accepted state of the art for showing that software meets the MDR's and IVDR's requirements for software life cycles. It sits on two foundations it requires but does not repeat: a quality management system (ISO 13485) and risk management (ISO 14971).

    Three safety classes

    The manufacturer classifies the software system by the worst harm a failure could contribute to, assuming the software will fail: the standard takes the probability of a software failure as 1, because it cannot be estimated the way a component failure rate can. The only way down is a risk control measure outside the software, such as an independent hardware limit, that keeps the resulting risk acceptable.

    • Class A: no injury or damage to health is possible.
    • Class B: non-serious injury is possible.
    • Class C: death or serious injury is possible.

    A system can be divided into software items with different classes, but only if the architecture shows they are effectively segregated, so a failure in one cannot corrupt another.

    Five processes

    The standard's requirements fall into five processes. Development (clause 5) runs from planning and requirements through architecture, detailed design, unit implementation and verification, integration, system testing, and release. Maintenance (6) applies the same discipline to every change after release. Risk management (7) traces how software can contribute to hazards and verifies each software risk control. Configuration management (8) identifies every version of every item, and problem resolution (9) records, investigates, and closes every defect report.

    Most modern devices contain code the manufacturer did not write: an operating system, a database, an open-source library. IEC 62304 calls this software of unknown provenance (SOUP). The manufacturer need not have its source code, but must identify each SOUP item and version, specify what it relies on it to do, and evaluate its published list of known defects for effects on safety.

    Around 62304

    Several neighbors fill in what 62304 leaves out. IEC 82304-1 (2016) covers whole health software products that run on general-purpose computers, including validation and labeling. IEC 62366-1 covers usability engineering, and IEC 81001-5-1 (2021) adds security activities to the same life cycle. In the US, the FDA's 2023 guidance on premarket submissions for device software functions sets two documentation levels, Basic and Enhanced, based on risk; the Enhanced level asks for fuller design and testing documentation, in line with the higher 62304 classes.

    A second edition has been in preparation for years. A 2021 draft that would have replaced the three classes with two levels of process rigor was voted down. A new draft, which keeps the classes, widens the scope from medical device software to all health software, and adds security requirements, was still in committee in mid-2026, with publication not expected before 2028.

    Part II

    Europe, and after the sale

    2017 Chapter 7 · The European Union

    From directives to regulations

    Europe took a different path. Under the "New Approach" of the 1990s, three directives, for active implantable devices (1990), medical devices (1993), and in vitro diagnostics (1998), set essential requirements, and manufacturers showed conformity themselves for low-risk products or through a notified body, a private certification organization designated by a member state, for higher-risk ones. A device bearing the CE mark could be sold in every member state. There was no central regulator like the FDA, and notified bodies competed for manufacturers' business.

    The weakness was exposed in 2010, when French authorities found that Poly Implant Prothèse had for years filled breast implants with cheap industrial silicone instead of medical grade, deceiving its notified body. Hundreds of thousands of women worldwide had received them. The scandal, together with problems with metal-on-metal hips, led to two regulations adopted in 2017: the Medical Device Regulation (MDR, 2017/745) and the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR, 2017/746). As regulations, they apply directly in every member state.

    What changed

    • Stricter classification. The MDR's 22 rules move many products up, including most software (Rule 11). The IVDR replaced a short list of high-risk tests with a rule-based scheme of classes A to D; about 80% of IVDs, which under the directive had needed no notified body, now do.
    • Clinical evidence. Manufacturers need clinical evaluation, and for high-risk implants it is checked by expert panels. Equivalence to another manufacturer's device is much harder to claim.
    • Notified bodies face tougher designation and unannounced audits.
    • Transparency and traceability. Unique device identifiers, and the European database EUDAMED. Its first four modules, covering economic operators, device registration, certificates, and market surveillance, became mandatory on 28 May 2026.

    The MDR's start was delayed a year by the COVID-19 pandemic, to 26 May 2021; the IVDR applied from 26 May 2022. The shortage of notified bodies and certificates threatened to remove devices from the market, so the EU twice extended the time legacy devices may be sold under their old certificates, if manufacturers are actively transitioning: Regulation 2023/607 for the MDR and Regulation 2024/1860 for the IVDR.

    1990 Chapter 8 · Postmarket surveillance

    Watching devices in use

    Premarket studies are small and short; some problems appear only after thousands of devices have been used for years. The Safe Medical Devices Act of 1990 required hospitals and other user facilities to report device-related deaths and serious injuries, and gave the FDA authority to order recalls and postmarket studies. Today, under 21 CFR Part 803, manufacturers must report deaths, serious injuries, and malfunctions that could cause them, generally within 30 days. The reports feed the public MAUDE database.

    When a device is defective, the manufacturer usually recalls it, and the FDA classifies the recall by hazard. Class I means a reasonable probability that use will cause serious adverse health consequences or death; Class II, temporary or medically reversible consequences, or a remote probability of serious ones; Class III, unlikely to cause adverse health consequences. In June 2021 Philips recalled millions of sleep apnea machines and ventilators whose sound-dampening foam could break down; the FDA classed it Class I. Since a 2013 rule, most devices carry a unique device identifier, which makes it possible to find which patients received which lots.

    Finding a signal

    Complaint data are noisy: counts vary month to month even when nothing has changed. Statistical process control offers tools to tell a real increase from chance. The cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart, introduced by E. S. Page in 1954, adds up each period's excess over a reference value and resets at zero, so small persistent shifts accumulate while random fluctuations cancel. For counts with a baseline rate \(\mu_0\), tuned to detect a rise to \(\mu_1\),

    \[ S_t = \max\!\left(0,\ S_{t-1} + x_t - k\right), \qquad k = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\ln(\mu_1/\mu_0)}, \]

    and an alarm sounds when \(S_t\) exceeds a threshold \(h\). A higher threshold means fewer false alarms but slower detection, the same trade-off as a diagnostic test's sensitivity and specificity.

    ISO Chapter 9 · Consensus standards

    The standards everyone cites

    Laws and regulations say what a device must achieve: safety, performance, adequate labeling. Standards, written by committees of manufacturers, regulators, clinicians, and test laboratories, say how to show it. They are voluntary in principle and close to mandatory in practice, because both jurisdictions give conformity with them special weight.

    In the US, the FDA Modernization Act of 1997 let the FDA recognize consensus standards. A manufacturer can submit a declaration of conformity with a recognized standard instead of full test reports, and the FDA keeps a public database of recognized standards and the editions it accepts. In the EU, standards adopted by the European standards bodies at the Commission's request become harmonised standards once their references are published in the Official Journal; under Article 8 of the MDR and IVDR, conformity with them gives a presumption of conformity with the requirements they cover. A manufacturer may take another route, but must then show it is at least as good.

    Chapter 10 · Today

    Converging, slowly

    The US and EU systems began from opposite ends, one with a central agency reviewing evidence and one with private bodies certifying conformity, and have been moving toward each other. The QMSR has aligned US quality rules with ISO 13485; the MDR has added clinical evidence requirements and expert panels that resemble FDA review; and both build on IMDRF documents written jointly by regulators from around the world.

    • Cybersecurity. Since March 2023, under section 524B of the FD&C Act added by the Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022, submissions for "cyber devices" must include a plan to monitor and fix vulnerabilities and a software bill of materials.
    • Artificial intelligence. The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) treats AI in medical devices that need a notified body as high-risk; its requirements for them apply from August 2027, alongside the MDR or IVDR.
    • Laboratory tests. With the LDT rule vacated, oversight of tests developed in laboratories remains with CLIA, and proposals for new legislation continue.
    • Supply. Europe's extended transition periods end between 2027 and 2029; whether notified-body capacity keeps up will decide how many legacy products remain available.

    The common thread

    Every rule in this volume answers one question: how much evidence is enough, given what could go wrong? The answer scales with risk, from a label and a clean factory for a tongue depressor to clinical trials and lifelong tracking for a pacemaker. The statistics are the same ones that run through earlier volumes: confidence intervals for a claim, Bayes' theorem for a test result, and sequential monitoring for a problem that has not shown itself yet.

    Glossary

    510(k)
    A US premarket notification showing a device is substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate.
    CE mark
    The marking that shows a product conforms to EU requirements and may be sold throughout the EU.
    CLIA
    The Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988, which regulate US clinical laboratories.
    De Novo
    A US pathway to classify a novel low- or moderate-risk device with no predicate into Class I or II.
    Design controls
    Documented procedures governing a device's design inputs, outputs, reviews, verification, validation, and changes.
    Harmonised standard
    A European standard whose reference is published in the Official Journal; conforming to it gives a presumption of conformity with the requirements it covers.
    EUDAMED
    The EU database for devices, economic operators, certificates, vigilance, and clinical investigations.
    IVD
    In vitro diagnostic: a device that examines specimens taken from the body.
    LDT
    Laboratory-developed test: a test designed, manufactured, and used within a single laboratory.
    PCCP
    Predetermined change control plan: a pre-authorized description of planned device modifications and how they will be validated.
    PMA
    Premarket approval, the US pathway for Class III devices, based on evidence of safety and effectiveness.
    Predictive value
    The probability that a positive (or negative) result is correct; it depends on prevalence.
    SaMD
    Software as a medical device: software that is a medical device without being part of hardware.
    Software safety class
    IEC 62304’s A, B, or C rating of software by the worst harm its failure could contribute to.
    SOUP
    Software of unknown provenance: software not developed for the device under IEC 62304, such as an operating system or open-source library.
    Special controls
    Device-type-specific requirements, such as performance testing and labeling, for US Class II devices.
    UDI
    Unique device identifier, a code on the label that identifies a device and its production lot.
